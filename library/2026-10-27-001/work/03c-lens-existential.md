## Existential reading of Khadija Amrani

### Reading

The given pressing hardest is **freedom**, and retirement is taking away the shelter she built against it. At 23:10 on 14 October 1981 she stood under a café awning and chose. For forty-four years she has told it as something that simply happened: "When someone goes, they're gone." "It's how it was." That is **bad faith** in its purest form. She turned a choice into a family custom, and she inherited the custom from a mother who folded card over a sister's face.

Her defence is **specialness through competence**. She is the fastest scanner, short only twice, the woman who catches thieves without looking up. The till is her **immortality project**: a perfect record, a view of the whole floor at 3 a.m. "like being on a roof," the last roof she shared with Fatiha. In 21 days the project ends. The pension "doesn't get bigger," and the day arrives with "nothing to count."

Fatiha is the **double**, but the mechanism works differently here than the library file suggests. This double is not an unlived self that would have been better. Fatiha took the bus and still ended up with bad knees and a longing for the hammam in Fès. The road taken led home too. What Fatiha carries is Khadija's disowned *authorship*. And Khadija has not been ambushed by this double. She has kept it in tissue, and she is now arranging the meeting herself, coin by coin.

She despises "people who stand in the doorway." She has been standing in one since 1981.

**The question under the story: Is it too late to go?**

### Evidence

- The bus left at 23:42 while she watched from across the road. Nobody noticed she had gone out (dossier: formative scene B).
- "When someone goes, they're gone" (interview: Q6). "Which side the silence started from" (interview: Q16). Fatiha telephoned twice, and Khadija sent Zohra down to say she was out (dossier: §10).
- "If you're going, go… Easy to say to other people" (interview: Q9). She gave Rachid the same advice (dossier: §10).
- The fastest scanner, short twice in 27 years. "Not a thief. Whatever else," said while turning an earring (interview: Q2, Q10). The earrings were bought with Fatiha's 190 dirhams (dossier: §11).
- "I'm afraid of being seen. From behind" (interview: Q13). Fatiha used to write on her back (dossier: formative scene A).
- Her phone searches: hammam hours, meter rates, the night CTM bus, how to stretch shoes (dossier: §7). Her note to herself to check the jar for two-dirham coins (interview: walking away).
- The rule of Egyptian films: "the person on the platform… one more chance… In life I don't know the rule" (interview: Q23).
- She has asked Hamid twice about part-time night cover (dossier: §10).

### Under pressure

**First move:** logistics. She counts coins, complains about the meters, and finishes other people's sentences. Freedom becomes a parking problem she can solve.

**Escalation:** she drives to Bab Boujloud in her slippers and feeds the meter. Then she sits in the Dacia for exactly the paid time and drives home. This is the awning again, now with a receipt, which she folds in four. She may take the long way round *through* Mohamed's street and walk past his stall without stopping.

**Worst five minutes:** she is in the changing room of the hammam with her djellaba off. Her shoulders are bare for the first time since 1981, the left one lower. She cannot go forward into the hot room, and dressing again would mean admitting she came. A woman's laugh comes from the steam. She does what has always saved her and becomes the cashier. She tells the attendant the exact price of the scrub, counts the change twice, and talks about tomatoes. The cruellest version has her running back to the store and asking Hamid for nights. That would be choosing the role so she doesn't have to choose herself.

### Tells

- When a question gets close, she folds whatever paper is nearest into four and presses the crease with her thumbnail.
- When anyone says "thief," "going," or "owe," she turns her left earring between finger and thumb.
- She stops and watches anyone who pushes their sleeves up past the elbow, longer than she watches anything else in the room.
- She answers a personal question with a price or a number.
- In every café and waiting room she chooses the chair with its back to the wall.

### Arc

The change open to her is not reunion. Reunion would be the **ultimate rescuer** of the Egyptian films: the last-reel chance handed to the person on the platform. The authentic change is to *go* whether or not Fatiha is there. She would have to walk in, undress, and let her back be seen and touched. That means owning the girl under the awning as herself, not as custom, fear, or "how it was."

It would cost her the story that she is not a thief, the comfort of being the one who never comes up short, and perhaps the earrings.

Not changing looks like this. The voucher expires, folded in four. She takes the part-time nights, and Youssef's thermos goes cold on a table nobody sits at. Leïla keeps drawing the handbag bigger.

**Ending image:** in the hot room she pushes up sleeves she isn't wearing, and turns to the back beside her. Nobody needs to say a word.

### What my lens can't explain

- **Why she didn't board.** My lens insists that freedom has no cause, which is honest but leaves the writer without a motive. The material never says whether the reason was fear, family, her body, or what she felt for Fatiha. The erotic charge here (the sleeves, the shared lipstick, the shared mattress) is better read by other lenses.
- **The earrings.** She turned the stolen money into something she wears every day, against her face. This is neither concealment nor plain guilt. It may be a confession worn in public, and my lens can only guess at that.

### Confidence

High. The doorway, the bus, and her own "If you're going, go" line up with unusual precision, and her phone searches show her already moving toward the double herself.
