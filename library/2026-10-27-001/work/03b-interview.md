## Interview with Khadija Amrani

**1. Tell me a bit about yourself. Who are you?**

Who am I. *Wallah*, you start with the hard one. I'm Khadija Amrani. Sixty-three. Fassia, born in the medina, Talaa Kebira, a house with a fig tree in a tub, not even in the ground. I live in the ville nouvelle now, near Mohammed V, third floor, no lift. My knees know the number of stairs better than I do. At work they call me Lalla Khadija. The manager calls me Hajja. I have never been to Mecca. I'll come back to him, don't worry.

What else. I'm a cashier. Twenty-one days left. Twenty-one nights, really, because I work nights. After that I'm a, what do you say, *retraitée*. A pensioner. *Safi*. That's who I am.

**2. What do you do for a living, and are you good at it?**

Cashier, Supermarché Al Wifaq, by the train station. Twenty-seven years. Nights since '98.

Am I good at it? *Ha*. Ask the computer. The store makes figures, scans per minute, and I am the fastest. Not "one of." The fastest. The young ones have phones in their pockets and fingers like sausages. I know every produce code. All hundred and forty. Courgette, coriander, the little mandarins, the big mandarins. I give the change before the machine tells me. A man gives me a hundred for seventy-three fifty, I have the twenty-six fifty in my hand while the screen is still thinking.

Twenty-seven years, my drawer was short twice. Twenty dirhams in 2003, five dirhams in 2017. I still don't know where those five dirhams went. It bothers me more than it should.

The pay is fine. Around five thousand. [lie: the true figure is about 4,150 a month with the night premium] It's enough. You don't do this for the money. Well, you do. But not only.

**3. Describe a normal day.**

My day is upside down. You want to start at night or morning?

Six o'clock I hand over my till. I count the drawer twice. Always twice. Then I drive home. I have slippers in the car, I change into them, the work shoes kill my feet. Home at twenty to seven. My husband is at the table with his newspaper, and there's tea. We talk. Tomatoes, they're nine dirhams now, can you believe it. His blood test. Then he goes to his café and I wash the glasses and I sleep. Seven-thirty to one-thirty, curtains pinned shut with clothes pegs, because those curtains, they let in light from the side like a sieve.

Lunch, lentils, whatever there is. He does his crossword. Then errands, the pharmacy, the bakery, sometimes the bank. I like to see the number with my own eyes, not on a phone. Four o'clock I call my sister. She talks, I listen, twenty minutes, she could do two hours. Then I play cards on the phone a little, I nap, I eat something light, I fill my flask with harira, and twenty past nine I'm in the car again.

Two in the morning I eat with Rachid, the security guard, in the stockroom. Three to five is the best time. Quiet. Taxi drivers, nurses, students who eat like birds that have lost their mother. I see the whole shop from my till at three o'clock. Every aisle. It's like being on a roof.

**4. Who are you closest to?**

My husband, of course. Youssef. Thirty-nine years. He's a good man, he's never once raised his voice. Every morning, a thermos of tea on the table, quarter past six, before I'm even home. Every morning.

My son, Amine, in Casablanca, and his little girl, Leïla. Six years old. She sends me voice notes, she sings, she tells me what she ate. I listen to them five times. My son too, he sends messages, I listen to all of them, every day. [lie: she has not opened his last voice note, which arrived nine days ago]

And at work... Naïma, she's at the till next to me. And Rachid. He calls me *mmi*. His own mother is alive, *hamdullah*, but he calls me *mmi*. I feed him. That's all it takes with men, you feed them.

My sister Zohra. Closest? She's... she's my sister. She knows me since she was born. That's not the same as close. That's more. Or less. I don't know.

**5. Describe your mother in three words. And your father.**

My mother. Hands. Tired. Behind. She embroidered for brides, *terz fassi*, and she was always two months behind on the orders. Always. Every bride in Fès was waiting for my mother.

My father. Radio. Buttons. Quiet. He sold buttons and zips and thread in the Kissaria. He sat with the radio. He had worries, money worries, everybody had them then. We didn't talk about it.

**6. What was your family like at dinner time when you were a child?**

Quiet. One dish in the middle, everybody eats from it. The radio on, football or Koran, depending on the night. My mother's bulb, one bulb, she embroidered under it until the last minute and then after. My job was to thread her needles. Six at a time, laid on a cushion, ready. I can still do it without glasses. Ask anyone.

Nobody talked much. You didn't talk at dinner, you ate. There were things you didn't say. My mother had a younger sister who went to Brussels and... *khlas*, we didn't say her name. There was a photograph on my mother's dresser of three girls and one face had a piece of card folded over it. A little square. You grew up with that card, you didn't ask. It's how it was. When someone goes, they're gone.

**7. What is something you're proud of that people don't know about?**

Hm.

My son did two years of private school, a BTS, after the bac. Expensive. I paid for it. Me. From the night premium. Every month, put aside. My husband thinks... I don't know what he thinks. That it came from somewhere. It came from me standing at a till at four in the morning.

And there's another one. Rachid's mother. She couldn't read, couldn't write, she signed papers with her thumb like in the old days. In 2008, every night at two o'clock in the stockroom, for six weeks, I taught her to sign her name. Not read, just sign. Her name. On the backs of receipts. She cried the first time she did it right. I said, "Don't cry on the receipts, they're for the accounts." She signs everything now. The bank, the *moqaddem*, everything.

Don't put that in the film with her name. She's proud.

**8. Who do you find unbearable? Why?**

*Ah*. Now we're talking.

Hamid. The manager. Thirty-four years old. Wait.

*(she straightens up, opens her palms, and her voice goes high and bright)* "Hajja! We need to be more *dynamique*! It's about the *process*, Hajja!"

*(back to herself, laughing; the gold tooth shows)*

Naïma cries when I do that. Cries. He calls me Hajja like I'm his grandmother. I've never been to Mecca. And one day he moved my till. My till. Half a metre to the left. "For flow." For flow! Half a metre to the left is my bad side, my hip, every bottle of oil I push, I turn on that hip. Twenty-seven years I'm in that spot, and a boy with a little beard like a pencil line moves me for flow.

He writes memos. Memos! About smiling. About bags. I keep them. Every one. For... I don't know what for. For evidence.

**9. What kind of person do you have no patience for?**

People who stand in the doorway.

You know the type. In the shop, in a house. They don't come in, they don't go out. They let the cold in. They hold the door and they think. At my till, people who unload half their shopping and then go back for yogurt. Men who can't decide between two packets of the same biscuit.

If you're going, go. If you're staying, stay. Don't stand there.

*(pause)*

That's what I told Rachid. He has a girl in Sefrou. He's been thinking about it for a year. I said, if you're going, go. Easy to say to other people.

**10. What is the worst thing someone could say about you? Why would they be wrong?**

That I'm hard. That I don't let anyone finish a sentence. That one's true, I'll give them that.

The worst? *(she touches one of her earrings, turns it)* That I take what isn't mine. That I'm a thief, let's say.

And they'd be wrong. Twenty-seven years, short twice. Twenty and five. In 2014 I caught a pickpocket, two women, working together, one with the phone cards, the other with her hand in a taxi driver's coat. I didn't even look up. "Sir, your wallet is in the lady's left sleeve." They gave me a certificate. "Vigilance Award." Framed.

I hung it in the staff toilet. Across from the sink. So everyone sees it when they wash their hands. That's the right place for it.

So, no. Not a thief. Whatever else.

**11. When did you last cry?**

Me? I don't remember. Years. I'm not a crier, my mother wasn't, my sister is. Zohra cries at advertisements for yogurt. [lie: she spent six minutes in the staff toilet after Naïma gave her the hammam voucher, three weeks ago]

Ask me when I last laughed. Last night. A boy tried to scan a melon as an onion on the self-check thing. A melon! As an onion! I said, *ya wlidi*, an onion is small, it makes you cry. A melon is big. Look at it.

**12. What do you do when you're angry?**

I scan faster.

At home, I wash the glasses. Even if they're clean. You put your hands in the water, you rub, it passes. Youssef knows. If he hears glasses, he goes to the café.

I don't shout. Shouting is for people with time.

**13. What are you afraid of? Not spiders. Really.**

*(long pause)*

The day. The whole day. Being at home in the day, awake, every day, with nothing to count.

Twenty-seven years I'm awake when the city sleeps. It's mine, the night. Now Youssef says, "You'll be home now." Like it's a present. Home. In the salon with the television on mute. *(shrugs)* He's a good man. It's not him.

And... my back. Silly thing. A physio once took a picture of my back on his phone, to show me how I lean, one shoulder down, the hip. I made him delete it. I watched him do it. I don't need to see that. I know what it looks like. I've been carrying it.

What else. You said really. I'm afraid of being seen. From behind. *(laughs)* That sounds strange. Write down "the day." Write that.

**14. What would you do with a completely free year and enough money?**

Free year? I'm about to have twenty of them, *inshallah*, without the money. Two thousand two hundred and forty a month, the CNSS says. I calculated it four times. It doesn't get bigger.

With money... I'd go to the sea. The north. Tangier, maybe. I've never been to Tangier. Imagine. Sixty-three years in Fès, never once.

Or I'd go sit in Casablanca with Leïla and let her paint my nails. She draws me with a very big handbag. It's on my fridge. A very big handbag. She's not wrong.

I'd buy shoes that fit. That's a joke. Half a joke.

**15. What do you want that you haven't got?**

A bigger pension, write that, maybe the minister watches documentaries.

*(beat)*

Time back. Everybody says that. Not the whole time, just... one evening. One hour. Twenty minutes.

No. Shoes that fit. Leave it there.

**16. Is there someone you've never forgiven?**

Hamid, for the till. *(smiles)* No, that's small.

My mother never forgave her sister. The one with the card on her face. For going. For not writing. I used to think that was strong of my mother. Now I think... maybe the sister did write. Maybe somebody didn't answer. You don't know, when you're a child, which side the silence started from.

Me? No. I don't keep things. I'm not the type.

**17. Is there something you've never forgiven yourself for?**

The five dirhams. 2017. *(laughs, then doesn't quite)*

I'm serious, a little. I counted that drawer three times. Five dirhams. Somebody walked off with five dirhams of mine, or I gave it to them without knowing, and I have never found out.

*(she takes the paper cup's sleeve off the table, folds it in four, corner to corner, presses the fold flat with her thumbnail)*

Something else, yes. There is. I'd rather not talk about that. Next question, *aafak*.

**18. Tell me about the love of your life. Or someone you thought was.**

The love of my life. *(she laughs)* You people.

There was a boy. Tarek. 1980. He sold cassettes at Bab Ftouh, Abdelhalim, Oum Kalthoum, the Egyptians. I spoke to him three times. Three. That was a great romance in 1980, three conversations. Naïma asked me once, and that's what I told her.

Then my aunt found me Youssef. An Arabic teacher. Good family, steady job. We married in '86. We had our son. *Hamdullah*. That's love too, a thermos at quarter past six. People think love is the films. Love is someone who leaves the tea.

*(pause)*

When I was young I had a friend. A girlfriend. We did everything together. Slept on the roof in summer, shared one lipstick, she kept it at her house because her mother didn't check. That's not what you mean. That's... a friend. She went away.

Tarek. Write Tarek.

**19. What do you find attractive in a person? Be specific.**

Specific. *(considers)*

When they push their sleeves up before they start a job. Up past the elbow. A mechanic, a man at the bakery about to knead, anybody. They don't even think about it, they just, *(she mimes it, one arm then the other)* like that. And then they start.

I'll stand in the garage and watch that longer than I'll watch the television. My husband thinks I'm checking they don't cheat me on the oil change. *(laughs)* I am. Also.

It means someone is about to do something properly. With their whole arm. That's all.

**20. What do you do when nobody is watching?**

I play solitaire. Four thousand, one hundred and something games. The phone counts them, it's shameful.

I do my impressions to the bathroom mirror. Hamid. The CNSS clerk, she has a voice like a door. The drunk who comes every Thursday and asks me to marry him. The mirror only shows me from here up *(hand at the collarbone)*, that's enough for impressions.

I say the lines in the old Egyptian films before the actors. Faten Hamama, I know her better than my cousins.

And sometimes I sit with the television on mute and I don't watch it. That's also a thing I do.

**21. What do you collect, keep or can't throw away?**

Nothing. I'm not sentimental. Ask Youssef, I throw away his newspapers before he's finished with them.

Well. Hamid's memos, I told you, in a bag in my locker. That's not sentimental, that's legal.

A notebook of debts. People at night who are one dirham short, two dirhams. I write it down. I've never collected one. Not once. Twenty-seven years of one dirham. Maybe I'll frame it.

My son's football pennant is still on the wall in my room. It was his room. I sleep there now. I didn't take it down. That's laziness, not sentiment.

*(beat)*

And old things. Some things under the bed. Everybody has something under the bed. Shoes. Old shoes. Never mind.

**22. What would the people who know you best say is your biggest flaw?**

Rachid would say I never let him finish a sentence. He's right. I already know how his sentences end. He's twenty-nine, his sentences are not complicated.

My sister would say I don't say things. She says, "Khadija, you keep it all in a box." *(pause)* That's her expression.

Naïma would say I'm stubborn. Youssef wouldn't say anything. That's his flaw. Not mine.

**23. If your life were a film, what genre would it be?**

Egyptian. Black and white. The kind I watch at five in the afternoon.

A comedy, I'd like to say. Melons and onions. Hamid saying *dynamique*.

But those Egyptian films, they always have a train or a bus leaving. Somebody at the window, somebody on the platform, music up. And the person on the platform, they always have one more chance, later, in the last ten minutes. That's the rule in those films. In life I don't know the rule.

Comedy. Put comedy.

**24. What do you think will happen to you in the next year?**

On the twenty-eighth there's a party. Hamid ordered a cake. He sent me a message with a smiling face. I haven't answered. What do I say, "thank you for the cake, give me back my half-metre"?

Then I'll be home. Youssef wants to sell the car. My son wants me to come live in Casablanca. Everyone has plans for me. I asked Hamid if they need someone part-time at night, just in case. Don't put that in, Youssef doesn't know.

*(she turns the earring again)*

I might go to the hammam. Naïma gave me a voucher. Bab Boujloud, the women's hours, a scrub included. It expires at the end of the month. I haven't been to a public hammam in... a long time. Years. They've put parking meters there now, coin ones, outside Bab Boujloud, can you believe it? Coins! Who carries coins? Well. I do, I have a jar in the car. But still.

What will happen. *Allahu a'lam*. God knows. I'll wake up in the day. That much I know.

**25. Is there anything you'd like to ask me?**

Yes. How much do they pay you for this?

*(laughs)* No, I'm joking. I don't care.

Do you know: if you have a pair of leather shoes that are one size too small, can you stretch them? Really stretch them? My phone says put them on with thick wet socks and walk around. Or a machine, at the cobbler. I don't trust the phone.

They've never been worn. That's why. They were too small from the start. Someone said they'd stretch.

Never mind. You're not a cobbler. Thank you, *a sidi*. Are we finished? I have to fill my flask.

## Walking away
Nice boy, too many questions about love, they always want love. I should check the jar in the Dacia for two-dirham pieces. Those meters at Bab Boujloud won't take anything else.
