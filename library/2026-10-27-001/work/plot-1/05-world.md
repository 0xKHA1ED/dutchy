## Places

### Outside Bab Boujloud: the kerb and the meter
Used in scenes: 1.1, 4.3, 4.4

Demands / forbids: It demands exact payment and speed: pay, go, come back. It forbids staying. The sticker allows one hour and no more, whatever you put in.

Power: The meter and the private company behind it. A man in a company vest walks the row with a handheld printer. He looks at number plates, never at drivers. He watches the way Khadija watches at the till: objects, not faces. He appears once in 1.1, walking away. In 4.3 he has already been.

Pressure on the mechanism: This is the one place where her rules work perfectly. Coins go in, minutes come out, and nobody looks at anyone. It gives the Counter a clean, external reason to leave any room. Because it sits outside the walls, every step deeper into the hammam is also a step away from her excuse.

Strange detail: When the meter is broken, the company covers its head with a plastic bag. Today the bag has been pushed back and tied round the post, like a headscarf slipped down to the neck.

Sound anchor: The *clac* of each two-dirham coin falling inside the meter, one coin, one clac, minutes appearing on the little screen.

Other specific details:
- Her car is an old white Fiat Uno with a crack across the dashboard. A Nescafé jar of two-dirham coins sits in the cup holder, and sunflower-seed shells collect in the door pocket.
- A man sells roasted sunflower seeds in paper cones folded from school exercise books.
- Red petits taxis wait in a line, their drivers leaning on the bonnets and talking across them.
- At the café opposite, the men's chairs all face the street, as if it were a television.
- The sticker is in Arabic and French: *pièces de 2 DH uniquement / 1 heure maximum / amende forfaitaire*, with one price. Suggestion: keep the flat fine at a round, plausible sum (e.g. 100 dirhams) so that the "night bus to Tangier" joke and the 4.3 total match. Sticker and ticket must show the same number. If unsure of real Fès practice, keep it as an ordinary private-firm ticket under the wiper and avoid a clamp.

### The hammam door and changing area
Used in scenes: 1.2, 4.1

Demands / forbids: It demands undressing in front of strangers and leaving your clothes and shoes in trust. It also demands patience at the door. It forbids hurry and private corners. There are no lockers. Your bundle sits on a bench in everyone's view.

Power: The desk woman, who sits on a raised platform above the benches. She takes the money, sells the small things and watches the clothes. She is a professional watcher seated higher than everyone, the job Khadija does at night. She does not look up when Khadija pays.

Pressure on the mechanism: Khadija must uncover her body and, crossing the room, show her back. It is the only time in the story she does so willingly. The small mirror shows her only from the collarbones up, the exact inverse of how she watches others. The shoes at the door put her secret pair, the twin pair and her real feet in one line.

Strange detail: A nail by the desk holds lost things: a child's sock, a key on red string, three single earrings, none of them gold. Use it once in 1.2 as colour. Don't return to it, and never let Khadija comment.

Sound anchor: The desk woman drops each entry fee into an old biscuit tin she uses as a cash box. It makes a dull, tinny clink, not the clean *clac* of the meter. (This secretly plants the tin of the 4.2 flash. It must never be connected aloud.)

Other specific details:
- The wet slippers stand in a crooked line on a sheet of flattened cardboard laid over the wet floor.
- A shelf behind the desk holds plastic bags of black soap, sachets of shampoo, and new gloves still in their wrappers.
- Clothes are left tied into bundles with their owner's own scarf.
- The mirror is small and hung high for tying scarves, so it stops at the collarbones.
- The heavy door has a rubber strip at the bottom that sighs when it closes.

### The cold room (la salle froide)
Used in scenes: 1.3, 1.4

Demands / forbids: It demands that you claim a place quickly, with a bucket, a stool or a bag of things. It forbids staring openly, though everyone sees everything. Talk is free and loud.

Power: The women who came first, whose buckets mark territory, and the attendant. Souad decides who is next and where the water goes. She shows it physically by standing in the doorway, arms folded, holding the only spare bucket.

Pressure on the mechanism: It is the perfect room for the Detective. It is cool, wide and bright enough to see hands, feet and purses from a distance, and her back can stay against the wall. Her method works best here, which is why the case is born here.

Strange detail: One red bucket has a phone number written on it in black marker, *so it comes home*. Every other bucket is anonymous.

Sound anchor: Plastic buckets scraping on wet tiles, the sound echoing up into the dome.

Other specific details:
- Round glass holes in the dome drop small circles of daylight on the floor, and women step around them without noticing.
- Black soap sits in a cut-down margarine tub.
- A little girl carries a tin cup of water in both hands, very slowly.
- A plastic stool has one leg shorter than the others, and it knocks every time someone shifts.
- The clock over the door is a plain white kitchen clock in a plastic bag against the damp. (This is planted in 1.3, and Khadija never looks at it.)

### The warm room (la salle tiède)
Used in scenes: 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4

Demands / forbids: It demands closeness and patience. The code here is that women pour water over a neighbour's back, and offering to scrub a stranger's back is ordinary politeness. It forbids sitting apart for long without someone offering a hand.

Power: Whoever sits nearest the single tap fills first, and the buckets queue in a line. Souad moves through the queue at will. Dija holds a different power: the room laughs when she talks.

Pressure on the mechanism: The room's own manners keep offering Khadija what she refuses, a hand on her back. To stay a watcher here, she must keep refusing and eventually pay to refuse. The heat softens her body before she knows it, and she leans off the wall.

Strange detail: A sock is tied over the mouth of the tap as a filter, grey with age. The water comes through it softly.

Sound anchor: The tap filling buckets, always running. The sound changes as each bucket fills, rising in pitch toward the top, then the scrape of the full bucket pulled away and the next one pushed in.

Other specific details:
- Women sit on folded plastic mats brought from home.
- The air smells of henna and wet clay.
- A queue of buckets of different colours waits by the tap like a line of customers.
- Someone's mobile phone sits wrapped in a plastic bag on a high ledge, lighting up every few minutes and ignored.

### The hot room (la salle chaude)
Used in scenes: 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4

Demands / forbids: It demands slowness and surrender. You cannot hurry in this heat, and you lose your breath. It forbids keeping anything dry, a towel included, and it forbids distance. In the steam everyone is close.

Power: Nobody holds it, only the heat. The wall shared with the furnace is too hot to lean against. Whoever sits on the marble slab is the one being cared for.

Pressure on the mechanism: Her method fails here. The steam turns people into shapes, so details give way to voices and names. Her back cannot go against the wall because the wall burns, and the towel slides off. For the first time, the room watches her back rather than the other way round.

Strange detail: One drop falls from the dome onto the same tile, again and again, and has worn it paler than the others. Regulars leave that tile empty. Someone has put a plastic sandal on it to say *not here*.

Sound anchor: Water thrown on the hot floor hissing, and the slow drop from the dome landing on the pale tile. The drop is the room's clock.

Other specific details:
- The marble slab in the middle is worn into a soft hollow by years of bodies.
- The furnace behind the wall is shared with the bakery oven next door, and on some days the room faintly smells of bread.
- A metal cup lies on its side, too hot to pick up.
- Through the steam, the plain white clock over the door is the only sharp shape.

### The alleys up to the gate
Used in scenes: the walk down after 1.1 (offstage or one line), 4.2

Demands / forbids: They demand giving way, stepping aside and walking at the speed of the crowd and the mules. They forbid cars and forbid hurrying past anyone. The street is too narrow.

Power: Porters and their mules. *"Balak!"* means *move, now*, and everyone does, pressing themselves flat to the walls.

Pressure on the mechanism: Her escape route costs her body. Downhill was eleven minutes, and uphill on her hip is longer. The climb shows the listener her own walk, left foot flat. The mule forces her into her old position, back to a wall.

Strange detail: Outside the communal bakery oven, wooden boards of bread dough wait under cloths. Each one is marked with a family's sign, a cross, a dot or a bottle cap pressed into the dough, so the baker knows whose is whose.

Sound anchor: A porter's *"Balak!"*, then hooves on worn stone and the clank of gas bottles knocking together on the mule's back.

Other specific details:
- The cobbles are polished smooth by feet and slippery in the shade.
- Shutters rattle up as shops open late.
- A boy runs down with a tray of mint tea glasses balanced on one hand.
- A cat sleeps in a box of fresh mint at a vegetable stall.

## The story's sound

**A coin.** The story's heartbeat is the sound of a coin, or its absence. Khadija pays for everything, and the coin shows what she is buying each time.

- **1.1: the *clac* in the meter.** Each two-dirham coin drops with a clean *clac* and the minutes appear. It means control: time bought, exact and paid. The meter refuses the last coin, so there is no *clac*. She pockets it, and that coin is what the story follows.
- **1.2: the biscuit tin at the desk.** Her entry money falls into the desk woman's tin with a dull, tinny clink. It sounds like a different kind of money. The listener files it as colour. In 4.2 a biscuit tin returns in the flash.
- **1.2 → 2.3: the silent coin.** Through Chapter 1 and into Chapter 2, she counts the last coin by touch in her pocket, with no sound. In 2.3 she presses it into Souad's palm, and a coin given into a hand makes no noise. The writer can note it in five words (*la pièce ne fait aucun bruit*). This is payment that buys nothing but distance.
- **3.3: the coin on the counter, in her head.** She pushes the name *Fatiha* away like a coin slid across her till at 3 a.m., the small scrape of metal on plastic. Now the sound is imagined, and it means refusal.
- **4.2: coins that are not there.** Climbing, she counts with her fingers in an empty pocket. The flash gives a biscuit tin, and the writer may let it rattle once in memory, the sound of a shared box.
- **4.4: silence.** She reaches into the Nescafé jar for coins to feed a dead meter. She hears her fingernails on empty glass and nothing else, no *clac*. The story ends without the sound it began with.

Supporting clock sounds: the drip from the hot-room dome carries the counting inside the walls in Chapter 3, as the meter carries it outside. Never let the drip and the coin be compared on the page.

Notes for the writer:
- **La boucle** carries a free echo in French. The shoes have *une boucle dorée* (a gold buckle), and her hoops are *ses boucles d'oreilles en or*. Use the same word for both and never point it out.
- **Le ticket** is also free. The hammam receipt, the fine and her till are all *ticket*. Naïma's voucher is *le bon*.
- **Refrain in French:** *"Ton gommage est payé, lalla."* / *"Plus tard."*
- **Dija's line:** *"Doucement, doucement. Je suis un musée, pas un tapis."*
- **The 1.1 joke:** *"Même prix pour une minute ou pour un jour. Tu te rends compte."*
- **The rule:** *"Si tu pars, pars."*
