# Brief

## The story you are writing

**La Paire.** It is a crime story told in four chapters of about 1,000 words each (900 to 1,100), roughly 4,100 words in all. Write it in close third person on Khadija, in the past tense. The register starts comic and turns tragic, and the ending is bitter with one half-laugh left in it.

For the first two chapters, the listener is having a good time beside a sharp, exact, funny woman. Khadija is a night-shift cashier, three weeks from retirement. She has spotted a pickpocket team in a hammam in the Fès medina on women's day. A thin woman carries a heavy woman's purse from bench to bench and never opens it. She scrubs the heavy woman's back far too slowly, which must be a distraction. The listener wants the thief caught, enjoys Khadija's contempt, and trusts her eye, which really is expert.

In the hot room the fun drains away. The slowness starts to read as care. A name is said, and two women answer. The heavy woman begins to turn, and Khadija looks at the clock instead. The listener is relieved, because they did not want to see that face either. Then a parking ticket under the wiper shows that leaving cost exactly what staying would have cost. Her thumb goes to her gold earring, and the listener understands who the thief in the building was.

The heavy woman is only ever seen from behind. Khadija never lifts her eyes above anyone's collarbone, so the narration lives at her eye line: hands, purses, gloves, buckets, feet. No face is described anywhere in the story.

Direct address is allowed once at most, just before "Neuf minutes" in Chapter 3. It should be one short line in which the listener notices they have looked at a clock like that themselves. If it explains anything, cut it.

## What it is really about

*Never to be stated, in any form:* when we refuse a life, do we spend the rest of ours paying every small debt on time, so that we never have to see that life lived by someone else?

The story works only if the listener reaches this alone, at the car, doing the arithmetic on the ticket. Once anyone thinks it, says it, or comes close to it, the listener's own relief at "Neuf minutes" stops being theirs and becomes a lesson. Carry the question in coins, folds, and a thumb on an earring.

## The people

**Khadija Amrani, 63.** She will follow a stranger's back through every room in the building, as long as it never turns round. Here is what you need to know and never write out. In 1981, at nineteen, she and her friend Fatiha saved 380 dirhams in a biscuit tin. In a hurry and in the dark, they bought matching black flats with a gold buckle at the night market, for the night bus to Tangier. Khadija never got on. She kept the tin and spent Fatiha's half, 190 dirhams, on the gold hoops she wears every day. Nobody knows why she stayed, and that includes you, her, and the listener. Do not supply a reason.

What she does:
- **Her eyes stay below the collarbone.** She looks at hands, purses, gloves and feet, never above. This is the rule of your camera.
- **Her back stays against the tiles in every room,** with a towel over her shoulders until it is soaked and slides off.
- **She turns her left hoop** whenever a debt, "voleuse", money, or "payé" comes near. Never comment on this, not once.
- **She folds every paper in four,** corner to corner, and presses the fold with her thumbnail: the voucher, the hammam receipt, and finally the ticket.
- **She answers a feeling with a price or a time.** "Douze dirhams cinquante." "Vingt-six minutes."
- **She goes silent mid-sentence** when someone pushes their sleeves up past the elbow.
- **She counts coins by touch** in her djellaba pocket.

Her voice comes in short bursts, numbers, and fragments. She asks questions and answers them herself. She corrects herself: *« Vingt et un jours. Des nuits, plutôt. »* She never pauses long enough to be interrupted.

Keep the Darija untranslated in the French: *safi, wallah, khlas, lalla, aafak.* Her own phrases are *« Tu te rends compte. »*, *« Écris ça. »*, and *« Si tu pars, pars. »*

She uses *tu* with every woman in the hammam, as Darija does, and lets *lalla* carry the respect. She uses *vous* only in mockery, in her impression of her manager Hamid: *« un gâteau pour votre dynamique, Madame Amrani »*.

She never says the following:
- Fatiha's name aloud.
- "Tanger", except in one joke.
- *pardon*.
- *tu me manques*.
- Anything about her own back.

Her commentary runs under her breath, like a cashier at 3 a.m.:
- *« Personne ne frotte un dos aussi lentement. Personne. Sauf si l'autre main a un travail. »*
- *« Elle le porte comme des œufs. Comme des œufs ! Ce n'est pas son sac, je te le dis. »*
- *« Plus tard, lalla. Je suis occupée. Ton gant ne va pas s'envoler. »*

Her comedy is not a mask. She is genuinely funny, genuinely fair and genuinely kind. For twenty-seven years she has kept a notebook of strangers' one-dirham debts and never collected them. The listener should want her on their side at a till. The turn of the story is not that the jokes were fake. It is that the same eye that reads every back in the room refuses one back.

There are three ways she can be in a moment:
- **The Detective:** jokes, prices, the case. This one carries Chapters 1 and 2.
- **The Counter:** minutes, coins, *safi*. This one wins at the end.
- **A girl of fourteen on wet tiles,** who once felt a fingertip write slowly on her back and guessed the letters. She never speaks. She appears only as a body leaning a fraction off the wall toward a slow glove.

Small facts you can use:
- She has an old white Fiat Uno with a Nescafé jar of two-dirham coins in the cup holder.
- She is here on a gift voucher (*le bon*) from her colleague Naïma.
- She has a Vigilance Award from 2014, framed by her sink, for a purse-catch.
- Her manager Hamid has ordered a retirement cake.
- She knows one regular only as "the finger": a hand with a short fingertip. Bread and a lighter, two dirhams short, March, still in the notebook.
- Her henna is two weeks old and the grey shows at the parting.
- She arrives in black Velcro pharmacy sandals, size 39.
- Her unworn size-37 buckle flats are under her bed, still in tissue. They are never shown, only mentioned, once, as a joke.

**The thin woman (Fatiha Kaddour, 63).** Narration calls her *la femme mince* throughout. Her name enters Khadija's head once, in Chapter 3, and is pushed away. We see her only as Khadija sees her: hands, feet, and voice.

What she does:
- Before she takes the glove, she pushes up sleeves she is not wearing.
- Her scrubbing speed never changes when she is watched.
- She carries the purse like something fragile and never opens it.
- She recognised Khadija's walk in the cold room, the moment Khadija crossed it. Her head lifts once toward Khadija's wall and goes back down.

Her voice is fast, low and practical. Spanish slips out when she is tired: *« Venga, l'autre épaule. Je ne suis pas payée aujourd'hui, ne me fais pas demander deux fois. »* She scrubs backs for a living in Tangier, but nobody says so. Her shoes at the door are the buckle flats, size 38, resoled three times and soft at the heels. She says "Dija" once (Chapter 2, end) and "Khadija" once (Chapter 3).

**The heavy woman (Khadija Trabelsi, "Dija", 64).** She is seen from behind, always. You get the following:
- sloped shoulders, the left one lower;
- a stiff left hip;
- the left foot landing flat;
- henna-auburn ends over a grey parting;
- her voice.

She is loud and warm, and she works the room like a comedian, laughing at her own joke two words before the end. She sings under her breath when something hurts, and the worse it hurts, the brighter the song. She hands her purse back to the thin woman without looking, the way you trust a pocket. When "Khadija" is said aloud, she turns a full second late.

Her lines:
- *« Doucement, doucement. Je suis un musée, pas un tapis. »*
- *« Ah ! Là. C'est là qu'est la facture. »*
- *« Attention avec ça, ma nouvelle hanche est dedans. Elle est payée. »*

The purse holds a clinic deposit receipt for her hip operation. She teaches nothing. She is not a free spirit. She is a supervisor with a bad hip and a happy life, and she does not know Khadija is in the room. Never describe her from the front. Never say she looks like Khadija. Show the shoulder, the hip, the parting, and the size of her feet, and let the listener stack them up.

**Souad, 52, the attendant.** She keeps reappearing in doorways with the same bucket and towel, arms folded, unhurried. She tucks tips into the knot of her headscarf and counts them by touch. She lays her hand flat on a knee or a back, openly. That hand is missing a fingertip. She is "the finger" from Khadija's notebook. She has known Khadija's face from the till for years and decided not to say.

Her voice is direct and loud enough to cross three rooms, and she says less than she knows: *« Ton gommage est payé, lalla. »* She has one line that lands, in the hot room. She does not explain it, and she must never turn into the wise woman with the moral. If you want, give her one line of her own life: tips saved for her daughter's October train to Rabat.

Minor figures are yours: the desk woman on her raised platform, a woman dawdling in the doorway, a porter, and the man in a company vest who checks number plates and never drivers.

## The shape

The hammam's rooms set the shape: cold, then warm, then hot, then out. Each room is hotter and closer to the back, while the meter counts down outside the walls. Every chapter closes on a minute-mark and a step closer. The order of small beats, the dialogue, the gestures and the jokes are open water. Invent freely, as long as each chapter makes its turn and the clues below land where they are listed. The listed clues and turns are fixed.

The clock arithmetic is as follows. She parks at 60 minutes, walks 11 minutes downhill, arrives at 49, spots the pair at about 46, and Souad speaks to her in the hot room at about 20.

**Chapter 1: *Comme des œufs* (60 to 43).** The turn: from a day out to a case and a chase.

The listener should believe this is low-stakes comedy about a prickly, brilliant working woman. The only thing she thinks about is arithmetic. Do not open on retirement or loneliness. Open on logistics and jokes.

Clues to plant:
- **At the meter outside Bab Boujloud.**
  - She feeds it two-dirham coins from the jar.
  - The sticker says two-dirham coins only, one hour maximum, and a flat fine of 100 dirhams. She reads it like a price label: *« Même prix pour une minute ou pour un jour. Tu te rends compte. »*
  - She makes one price joke: for that fine you could take the night bus to Tanger. This is the only time "Tanger" is spoken in the whole story.
  - The meter refuses the last coin. She pockets it and counts it by touch.
  - Two dirhams calls up the finger, *still in the notebook*, and her thumb goes to her left hoop, without comment.
  - She folds Naïma's voucher in four and presses it with her thumbnail. *« Onze minutes. En descendant. »*
- **At the door.**
  - She says *« Si tu pars, pars »* to a woman blocking the doorway on her phone.
  - She lines up her Velcro 39s among the wet slippers, beside black buckle flats resoled into a little stack at the heel. Under her breath: *« La même boucle que les miennes. Les miennes, c'est du trente-sept. Sous le lit, encore dans le papier. On m'avait dit qu'elles allaient se faire. »* Make it a throwaway. The listener should file it as charm.
  - She pays, and the coin clinks into the desk woman's old biscuit tin. Never connect this tin to anything.
  - She folds the receipt in four.
  - She undresses with her back to the tiles and the towel on at once. The small mirror stops at her collarbones, and she gives it a cashier's nod.
  - To nobody: *« Soixante-trois ans. On regarde, on ne touche pas. Comme le musée Batha. »*
  - Crossing the cold room is the only time she willingly shows the room her back.
- **The case.** Within three minutes she has the pair.
  - *« Comme des œufs ! »*
  - The purse is never opened, and the thin woman pushes up phantom sleeves. Khadija goes quiet watching the sleeves.
  - The black soap is scooped from a tub: eight dirhams at Bab Ftouh.
  - She remembers the 2014 catch, *« Lalla, ton sac est dans la main de la mince »*, and turns her hoop at *voleuse*.
  - The thin woman's head lifts once toward her wall. *« Tu vois ? Une voleuse vérifie toujours où est le gardien. »*
  - A plain white clock hangs over every door. She never needs to look at it, because she has never once been wrong.
- **Souad in the doorway.** *« Ton gommage est payé, lalla. »* / *« Plus tard, lalla. Je suis occupée. »* Khadija sees only the bucket.
- **The move.** The heavy woman walks ahead into the warm room, her left foot landing flat. Khadija takes the bench directly behind.

Exit: *Quarante-trois minutes.*

**Chapter 2: *Un musée, pas un tapis* (43 to 24).** The turn: from sport to something older pulling at her body, and a name that is half of her own, covered with a number.

The listener laughs harder than at any point in the story, then catches the first real doubt that this is only a crime story.

Clues to plant:
- **The slow glove.** *« Personne ne frotte un dos aussi lentement. »* From behind she sees henna-auburn over a grey parting and a left shoulder lower than the right. A back, only a back.
- **Her shoe-size game.** The thin woman is *« trente-huit, étroit »*. The heavy woman is *« trente-neuf, comme un bateau »*. She says nothing about her own feet.
- **The thin woman.** She pushes up the phantom sleeves again, and Khadija stops mid-sentence. Then: *« Venga, l'autre épaule. Je ne suis pas payée aujourd'hui… »* (no name here). Khadija hears a con artist's wink: *« Pas payée aujourd'hui. Écris ça. »*
- **The museum joke.** Dija: *« Doucement, doucement. Je suis un musée, pas un tapis. »* The room laughs. Khadija laughs under her breath, the gold molar almost showing, then stops. She hardens the case to cover it: *« Une femme qui rit comme ça ne surveille pas son sac. C'est pour ça qu'elles l'ont choisie. »*
  - Optional, from Dija to the room: at home she just walks into the sea and lets it do this.
- **Souad returns.** *« Ton gommage est payé, lalla. »* Then: *« Le gant va attraper tes boucles d'oreilles. Enlève-les. »*
  - *« Non. »* Khadija turns the left one.
  - Souad: *« Du vieil or. On ne fait plus ce jaune. »*
  - Khadija, flat, still turning it: *« Cent quatre-vingt-dix. »* The listener hears cashier reflex.
  - Khadija presses her last coin into Souad's palm without looking at the hand. Her eyes stay on the purse. *« Plus tard, aafak. Va frotter quelqu'un qui en a envie. »*
  - The coin makes no sound. Souad tucks it into her headscarf knot, above Khadija's eye line.
- **The pull.** The glove moves lower and slower, and Khadija leans a fraction off the wall without knowing it.
  - A flicker comes: wet tiles at fourteen, a fingertip moving slowly on her back, letters she guessed. There is no word, no person, and no meaning, only one drifting sentence cut off hard: *« Trente minutes. Vingt-neuf. »*
  - Her towel is heavy now.
- **The name.** Dija stands and holds the purse out behind her without looking. *« Attention avec ça. »* / *« Oui, Dija. »* Khadija's commentary misses a beat. Write only the gap.
- **The move.** They go toward the hot room, Dija slow on the stiff left hip. Khadija rises, one hand going to her own hip.

Exit: *Vingt-quatre minutes.*

**Chapter 3: *Deux femmes répondent* (24 to 9).** The turn: from the case to a name she knows, and her eyes go to the clock.

The register is eerie, then tragic. Khadija speaks less than in any other chapter, and her commentary dies.

Clues to plant:
- **The towel.** It soaks through and slides onto the tiles. She reaches once, then leaves it. Her back is bare to the room.
- **The purse.** Dija: *« ma nouvelle hanche est dedans. Elle est payée. »* The hoop turns at *payée*. The Detective tries, *« Elles disent toujours payée »*, and it comes out weaker. The thin woman's hand goes under Dija's elbow and lowers her onto the hot marble.
- **Souad, at about 20.** She sits beside Khadija, her hand flat on her own knee at Khadija's eye height. *« Tu regardes son dos depuis une heure. Tu veux que je te dise à quoi ressemble le tien ? »*
  - Khadija, at once: *« Vingt-six minutes. Pas une heure. »* Let it get its laugh.
  - Then she sees the hand. *Le doigt. Deux dirhams, dans le carnet.* The coin in the knot was already owed to her.
  - No "Plus tard" comes. Souad waits, stands, says *« Ton gommage est payé. Je ne vais nulle part. »*, and goes.
- **The song.** The glove nears the hip, and Dija sings brighter and brighter. *« Ah ! Là. C'est là qu'est la facture. »*
- **The name, at about 12.** The thin woman says *« Khadija »*, low, to the back she is working. Give the name its own line.
  - Two women answer. Dija answers a full second late, *« Na'am ? »*, without turning. Khadija's mouth is already open, and she closes it on nothing.
  - *Fatiha* rises in her head, once. She pushes it back like a coin slid across a counter. *Next.*
- **The turn of the back.** The glove stops. The heavy woman begins, slowly, on the stiff left hip, to turn, the lower left shoulder coming round first.
  - Khadija's eyes rise above the collarbone for the only time in the story. They go past the shoulder, up the wall, to the white clock over the door.

Exit: *Neuf minutes.* The listener feels relief.

**Chapter 4: *Même prix* (9 to −6).** The turn: from escape to a wrong account, perfectly closed. See the ending below.

## The ending

This is fixed in substance. The wording is yours, except for the last line.

**1. The door.** She comes out fast, her eyes back at feet. She passes the buckle flats: size 38, the number worn half off the insole, soft at the heels. She steps into her 39s, bending no more than her hip allows. Three sizes stand in one doorway, and no sentence connects them.

**2. Dressing.** She pulls the djellaba over wet skin, and it sticks to her unscrubbed back. She ties the scarf over dripping hair. Through the wall, Dija is singing brightly. Khadija doesn't turn toward it. Under her breath: *« Si tu pars, pars. »*

**3. The flash.** As the street door opens, it comes in fragments: nouns and numbers, no verbs, no "je", no name, no bus. *Une boîte à biscuits. Trois cent quatre-vingts. La moitié. Cent quatre-vingt-dix. Un comptoir en verre. Un plateau de velours.* It cuts off there.

**4. The climb.** She goes uphill on the bad hip, her left foot landing flat, and nobody comments. She counts steps against minutes aloud, under her breath. *« Balak ! »* A mule comes down loaded with gas bottles, and she flattens her back against the stone to let it pass. She loses a minute and recounts. The listener should still be hoping she makes it.

**5. The car.** The ticket is under the stiff wiper. She lifts the wiper with two fingers, like something hot. She reads the ticket twice, the way she reads a price that scanned wrong. It hasn't. The printed time is four minutes after the meter ran out. She reads the sum aloud, flat, like a total at 3 a.m.: *« Cent dirhams. »* This is the sticker's sum. She does not say the joke. The listener does the arithmetic.

**6. Paying anyway.** She reaches through the window into the Nescafé jar for coins, to feed a dead meter for time already lost. Write her fingernails on empty glass. Her hand goes to her pocket, and there is nothing in it.

**7. The fold.** She folds the ticket in four, corner to corner, presses the fold flat with her thumbnail, and puts it in the empty pocket.

**8. The last line.** The same thumb rises, from habit, to the left hoop. It does not turn it.

> *Her thumb went to the left hoop, and stayed there.*

In French, keep it this plain: *Son pouce monta jusqu'à l'anneau gauche, et resta là.* (Or *la boucle gauche*, if you have used *boucle* for the earrings throughout. That echo of the shoe buckle is free and good. Never point it out.)

The last three sentences are actions, each shorter than the one before. There is no tear, no sky, no weather, no thought summing up the hour, and nobody understands anything out loud.

## The world

**Outside Bab Boujloud.** This is the one place where her rules work perfectly: coins in, minutes out, nobody looking at anybody. Each two-dirham coin falls with a clean *clac*, and the minutes appear on the little screen.

The meter's plastic bag, used to cover it when it is broken, has been pushed back and tied round the post, like a headscarf slipped down to the neck. Around her:
- red petits taxis, their drivers leaning across bonnets;
- a man selling sunflower seeds in cones folded from exercise-book pages;
- café chairs all facing the street like a television;
- sunflower-seed shells in her car door.

Light: hard white sun.

**The door and changing room.** There are no lockers. Clothes sit tied in their owner's scarf on benches in everyone's view, and the wet slippers stand in a crooked line on flattened cardboard. The desk woman sits higher than everyone, takes the money and watches the clothes, and she never looks up. Her cash box is an old biscuit tin with a dull, tinny clink.

A nail by the desk holds lost things: a child's sock, a key on red string, three single earrings, none of them gold. Use it once, as colour, and never let Khadija comment. The heavy door's rubber strip sighs when it closes. The mirror is small and hung high for tying scarves.

**The cold room.** Plastic buckets scrape on wet tiles, and the sound echoes up into the dome. Round glass holes in the dome drop small circles of daylight that women step around without noticing. Other details:
- one red bucket has a phone number written on it in marker, so it comes home;
- a stool with one short leg knocks whenever someone shifts;
- a little girl carries a tin cup of water in both hands, very slowly;
- the white kitchen clock hangs in a plastic bag against the damp.

This is the Detective's perfect room: cool, wide, everything visible, and her back to the wall.

**The warm room.** A single tap runs all chapter. A grey sock is tied over its mouth as a filter, and the tap's pitch rises as each bucket fills, then the full bucket scrapes away and the next one is pushed in. Buckets of different colours queue like customers. The air smells of henna and wet clay. A phone in a plastic bag lights up on a high ledge and is ignored. The room's manners keep offering her what she refuses, because here pouring water over a neighbour's back is ordinary politeness.

**The hot room.** Water hisses on the hot floor. One drop from the dome falls on the same tile, again and again, and has worn it paler than the others. Someone has set a plastic sandal on it to say *not here*. The drop is the room's clock. Do not compare it to the coin on the page.

The wall shared with the furnace burns, so she cannot lean on it. The marble slab is worn into a hollow by years of bodies. The furnace is shared with the bakery next door, and the room smells faintly of bread. Steam turns bodies into shapes, and only the white clock stays sharp.

**The alleys.** A porter's *« Balak ! »*, hooves on worn stone, gas bottles clanking together. The cobbles are polished and slippery in the shade. Boards of bread dough wait by the communal oven, each marked with a family's sign: a cross, a dot, or a bottle cap pressed into the dough. Shutters rattle up. A cat sleeps in a box of mint.

## Fixed points

- **Wildcard:** the heavy woman is seen only from behind, and no face is described anywhere in the story.
- **Eye line:** Khadija's eyes never go above a collarbone, except once, in 3.4, to the clock. The clock is planted in Chapter 1.
- **Names:**
  - "Tanger" is spoken once, in the Chapter 1 bus joke.
  - "Dija" is spoken once, at the end of Chapter 2 (*« Oui, Dija. »*).
  - "Khadija" is said once to the back in Chapter 3, and two women answer.
  - *Fatiha* occurs once, in Khadija's head, in Chapter 3.
  - Dija's joke has no name in it.
- **Refrain:**
  - *« Ton gommage est payé, lalla. » / « Plus tard. »* in Chapters 1 and 2.
  - In Chapter 3 it comes back without "Plus tard".
  - In Chapter 4 it is inverted: nobody offers her anything, and a ticket demands payment.
- **Echoes:** *« Même prix pour une minute ou pour un jour. Tu te rends compte. »* (Chapter 1) goes unsaid in Chapter 4, where the sum is read aloud instead. *« Si tu pars, pars »* goes from Chapter 1 to Chapter 4.
- **Minute-marks** close the chapters: *Quarante-trois*, *Vingt-quatre*, *Neuf*. The −4 is printed on the ticket.
- **Clue placements,** as listed in The shape:
  - the sticker and flat fine;
  - "Onze minutes. En descendant.";
  - the last coin;
  - the finger in the notebook, with the thumb to the hoop;
  - "sous le lit, encore dans le papier";
  - 38 and 39;
  - "Cent quatre-vingt-dix";
  - the coin into an unseen palm;

## Never say, never do

- **Never state the resemblance between the two women.** The listener builds it from the shoulder, the hip, the henna, the 39, and the museum joke. If you say it, the building is gone.
- **Never call the slow glove tenderness, or the "crime" care.** The moment the listener feels that themselves is the turn of the story.
- **Never say why she stayed in 1981.** Never show the bus, the awning, the night market, or any phone call. Her not knowing is part of what she refuses, and a reason would let her off.
- **Never say what was written on her back.** The flicker is touch only.
- **Never write a sentence that confesses the hoops,** and never comment on the earring-turning or on "cent quatre-vingt-dix". The last line has to do that work alone, by sending the listener back.
- **Never say she is afraid of being seen, or avoiding a face.** Show her back on the tiles, her eyes at feet, and the clock.
- **No *pardon*, no *tu me manques*, no love between the girls, and no *culpabilité*.** Each of these names a feeling the listener should be holding.
