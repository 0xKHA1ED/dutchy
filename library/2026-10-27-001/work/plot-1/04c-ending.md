## Ending type

**The choice, failed, with an ironic win inside it.** The temperature is bitter.

On the surface, Khadija fails at both of her stated wants. She does not catch the pair, and she does not beat the meter. Her real want is the one she never says: not to see the face. She gets exactly that, and she pays for it at a printed price. The fine is flat, so leaving bought her nothing. She could have stayed the whole hour for the same money.

The reframe is in the hoops. In a building full of suspected thieves, the only stolen goods were in her ears.

Both registers stay alive to the end. The listener should half-laugh at the fine, a cashier who reads a ticket like a wrongly scanned price, before the hoop lands.

## Final scene

*Chapter 4, about 1,000 words. Minute marks: 9 → −4.*

**1. The door (9).**
- Khadija comes out of the steam fast, her eyes back at their usual height: feet.
- The wet slippers stand in their line. Among them are the black buckle flats she noticed on the way in: size 38, the number worn half off the insole, resoled three times, soft at the heels where someone's feet pushed them out.
- She steps into her Velcro pharmacy sandals, size 39, without bending more than the hip allows.
- Nothing is said. The listener already knows from Chapter 1 about the size 37 pair in tissue under her bed, the pair that was "supposed to stretch." Three sizes stand in one doorway: the 37 that never walked, the 38 that walked to Tangier and back, the 39 her feet became anyway. No sentence connects them.

**2. Dressing (8 → 7).**
- She pulls the djellaba straight over wet skin. It sticks to her back, the unscrubbed back nobody touched.
- She does not dry her hair. She ties the scarf over it dripping.
- Behind her, through the wall, Dija's voice carries, singing brightly. The listener knows the brightness means pain. The glove has reached the hip.
- Khadija does not turn toward the sound. She mutters her own phrase to herself: *"If you're going, go."* This is planted in Chapter 1, said to a woman dawdling at the hammam door.

**3. The flash (7).**
- Numbers and objects only, in fragments, as she counts the coins that are no longer in her pocket: *A biscuit tin. Three hundred and eighty. Half. One hundred and ninety. A jeweller's glass counter. A velvet tray.*
- There is no name, no bus, no awning, and no sentence that says "I." The flash cuts off on the velvet tray as the street door opens.

**4. The alleys (7 → −6).**
- It is the climb back up toward the gate, the same alleys she came down in Chapter 1 timing herself: "Eleven minutes. Downhill."
- Now it is uphill on the bad left hip, with her left foot landing flat. The listener has watched that walk all story on someone else.
- She counts steps against minutes out loud under her breath, as the Counter.
- One concrete street interruption keeps the pull going: a porter's "*Balak!*" and a mule loaded with gas bottles. She presses herself flat against a wall to let it pass, back to the stone, which is her old position.
- She loses a minute and recounts. The listener should feel the race as real, still hoping she makes it, which is part of the complicity.

**5. The car (−6).**
- Outside the gate, the ticket is under the wiper. The wiper is stiff, and she has to lift it with two fingers like something hot.
- She reads the ticket twice, the way she reads a price that scanned wrong. It hasn't.
- The printed time is four minutes after the meter's last minute. Then she reads the sum aloud, flat, the way she reads a total to a customer at 3 a.m. It is the same sum as the sticker on the meter that she mocked in Chapter 1: *"Same price for one minute or one day. Can you believe it."*
- She does not say the joke now. The listener does the arithmetic: she could have stayed until the back turned, and seen the face, and paid exactly this.
- **Writer's note:** check current Fès practice. Fines by private parking firms or a clamp notice both work. What matters is that the fine is flat and that it was printed on the meter in Chapter 1.

**6. Paying anyway.**
- The paying instinct runs on. She reaches through the window into the coffee jar in the cup holder for coins, to feed the dead meter for time already lost. The jar is empty. She emptied it at 60.
- Her hand goes to the djellaba pocket where she counted the last two-dirham coin by touch. Nothing is there. The coin is in Souad's headscarf knot, and it was already owed to her.

**7. The fold.**
- She folds the ticket in four, corner to corner, and presses the fold flat with her thumbnail, as she did with the voucher and the receipt.
- She puts it in the empty pocket, where the coin was.

**8. The hoop.**
- The same thumb rises, out of habit, to the left earring. It does not turn it, as it has done every time the words *thief*, *owe* or *money* came near. It just stays.

**Not said, anywhere in the chapter:** Fatiha's name (it was used once, in Chapter 3); Tangier; why; sorry; anything about her back; anything about the face she didn't see. There is no tear, no horizon, no weather, and no thought that sums up the hour. Nobody understands anything out loud.

**One fix upstream to protect the ending:** in the skeleton's Chapter 2 line, cut "Fatiha" from Dija's joke. It should read: *"Slowly, slowly. I'm a museum, not a carpet."* That way the name lands only once, in Khadija's head, in the hot room.

## Last line

Candidates:
1. *Her thumb went to the left hoop, and stayed there.*
2. *Same price for one minute or one day.* This is the Chapter 1 meter joke, returned.
3. *Then she pressed the fold flat with her thumbnail, the way she closed a till.*

**Chosen: 1.**

- It is concrete and short, and it is an action rather than a verdict.
- It sends the listener back to Souad in the warm room: *"The glove will catch them. Take them off." "No."* The line was literal at the time. On replay, it becomes: Fatiha's glove would have caught the thief. Every earring-turn at "thief" and "owe" becomes a confession made with a hand.
- "Stayed" carries a faint echo of everything she never says about 1981, without naming it.
- Candidate 2 is the stronger refrain, but it is a punchline. It edges toward stating the moral ("she could have stayed"). It belongs to the listener's own arithmetic, so the sum is read aloud and the joke is left unspoken.
- Candidate 3 is just a description.

**The chapter-end rule.** This last chapter deliberately ends past the clock. The final minute mark (−4) comes a few lines earlier, printed on the ticket. Once counting has run out, the last "step closer" is the closest she ever gets to the truth: her thumb on the hoop.

## Alternative ending (not chosen)

**Comic collapse.** At nine minutes Khadija calls Souad over and makes her accusation in front of the hot room. The purse is opened: a clinic deposit receipt for a hip. The room laughs, and Dija laughs hardest, bent double and still facing away. Khadija stands dripping, earrings on, with the meter running out somewhere above the alleys and no one left to watch.

**Why not:**
- It gives the listener the public exposure scene the skeleton explicitly rejected.
- It turns a private refusal into a social humiliation, which lets Khadija off the hook. She is punished by the room, not by her own arithmetic.
- The laugh would replace the listener's quiet relief at "Nine minutes", and that relief is the recognition.

## Tests

**1. Last line: pass.**
- *"Her thumb went to the left hoop, and stayed there"* sends the listener straight back to the warm room: Souad's *"The glove will catch them. Take them off,"* Khadija's *"No,"* and her thumb turning the earring.
- From there the replay spreads to every earring-turn at "thief" and "owe", including the Chapter 1 joke about the 2014 purse-catch. That moment was the Detective at her proudest, and it was the thief touching her loot.
- It also reaches the first page. The ticket fold, and the empty pocket where the counted coin was, return to her feeding the meter at Bab Boujloud.

**2. Inevitability: pass.** A careful listener has all of these clues:
- The meter sticker says the fine is flat, so the clock was never worth the hurry.
- "Eleven minutes, downhill," so nine minutes up on that hip was always too few. Her choice at the clock was not arithmetic.
- She spends the meter's only usable coin to push away a hand on her back. The empty jar and pocket at the car are guaranteed.
- She refuses to remove the earrings, and turns them at "thief", "money" and "owe".
- The buckle flats at the door and her own pair "still in the tissue."
- Her eyes never go above the collarbone, so a turning face can only send them somewhere else, and the only other place she has ever looked for safety is a number.
- She folds every paper in four, so the ticket will be folded.
- Her stated rule, "If you're going, go."

Nothing depends on luck. The warden is ordinary city routine, and the ticket's printed time only confirms what the sticker promised.

**3. Deep question: pass.**
- The question is whether we pay every small debt on time so we never have to see the refused life lived by someone else. The ending answers yes, entirely through action.
- She leaves the back as it turns, choosing the meter over the face. She pays the coin to Souad, the fine to the city, and reaches for more coins to pay for time already lost.
- Then she closes the ticket perfectly, fold pressed with her thumbnail. The same thumb goes to the one debt she never pays and keeps it.
- The image that carries the answer is the thumbnail on the fold, then the thumb on the hoop: one account closed, the real one worn.
- The listener's relief at "Nine minutes", followed by the printed flat sum, shows them that the not-looking had a price, and that it bought nothing.
