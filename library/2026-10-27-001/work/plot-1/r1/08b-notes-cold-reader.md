## The four questions

1. About: A woman who has spent her life keeping tally of what others owe and watching for thieves spends the one hour she was given to be cared for watching two women's tenderness and reading it as theft. She refuses to be touched, and her vigilance costs her anyway.
2. In myself: It made me think of times I have filled a moment meant for me with judging other people, and how refusing a gift can be a way of not being seen. It also made me think of the private ledgers I keep of what people owe me.
3. Hit hardest: "— Khadija. / Deux femmes ont répondu."
4. At the last line: A quiet ache, and a grim appreciation that she folds the fine exactly as she folded Naïma's voucher, as if it were the same kind of paper. Under that was frustration: I sensed a lost person (Fatiha, the 190 dirhams, the earring held like a relic) whom I was never quite allowed to know, so the final thumb-on-earring moved me less than it wanted to.

## Hook

## Notes

1. [serious] "Vous aussi, vous avez déjà regardé une horloge comme ça." This is direct address at the exact climax. It tells me to recognise myself instead of letting the image do it, and it pulls me out of the steam at the one moment I most needed to stay. Direction: trust the image. Her eyes climbing past the turning shoulder to the clock is already the recognition, so the reader should arrive there without being addressed.

2. [serious] "*Fatiha.* / Le nom est monté, une fois." This name carries the hidden weight of the climax, but a cold reader has no anchor for it. I couldn't tell whether Fatiha was a lost friend, a sister, the girl who wrote letters on her back at fourteen, or whether the round woman *is* someone Khadija knows. So I couldn't tell whether she flees from seeing a face she knows or from seeing her own. Direction: keep the reticence, but give one earlier concrete thread tying this name to something already on the page, so that when it rises the reader feels it rather than decodes it.

3. [serious] "Une boîte à biscuits. Trois cent quatre-vingts. La moitié. Cent quatre-vingt-dix. Un comptoir en verre. Un plateau de velours." This is clearly the backstory of the earrings, and perhaps of "La Paire" itself, but compressed past legibility. I connected 190 to her line to the kessala, but not what was halved, or with whom. Coming right after the climax, it slows the exit. Direction: either seed one tangible image earlier (the tin, the counter) so this fragment completes a picture, or cut it and let the earring stand alone.

4. [serious] The opening three sentences are pure setting. Direction: let the opening carry tension from the first lines (the ticking hour, the refused coin, the voucher she doesn't want), so a reader with other things to do has a reason to stay before the detail starts paying off.

5. [minor] "— Vingt et un jours, a murmuré Khadija. Des nuits, plutôt. Et Hamid a commandé un gâteau." I assume this is retirement, but I had to guess. The Hamid voice is a detour that costs momentum just as the pair has hooked me. Direction: make the point of the twenty-one nights clear in one stroke, or cut the scene.

6. [minor] "Un seau rouge, avec un numéro de téléphone écrit au marqueur. […] Un tabouret qui boitait, toc" and "La mince, trente-huit. Étroit…" Observation catalogues establish her cashier's eye, but at length they become lists, and lists are where I skim. Direction: keep the sharpest one or two per passage, so her noticing stays a weapon rather than texture.

7. [minor] "Son pouce est monté à sa boucle d'oreille gauche et l'a tournée." This tic recurs about eight times before the last line. 8. [minor] "Deux femmes ont répondu. / La bouche de Khadija était déjà ouverte. […] Khadija a fermé la bouche sur rien." "Répondu" briefly contradicts the next lines, where she says nothing. I paused on the logic at the story's best moment. Direction: make sure the beat reads as her body answering, not her voice, so nothing snags.

## Protect

- "— Khadija. / Deux femmes ont répondu." This is the whole story in five words: the double revealed and the name that belongs to someone else's care. Do not soften it or explain it.
- "L'un d'eux était plus court que les autres. / Le doigt." The ledger of the first section closes on her. She has repaid the debt without knowing it, to the woman offering her what she refuses. It is an earned, enacted reversal.
- "Puis la mince a remonté ses manches. Elle n'avait pas de manches." This tells the reader that Khadija's reading is wrong without saying so, and shifts my attention from the "thief" to the watcher. Keep it, and keep her unfinished "Ça, c'est le geste d'une…"

## Scores

Hook: 2. The first three sentences are setting with no question ("Le pot de Nescafé était dans le porte-gobelet…"). The real hook, the 11 h 20 deadline, arrives a page later. To reach a 4, the opening would need to plant the clock or the refused care immediately.

Momentum: 3. The countdown structure ("Quarante-trois minutes." / "Vingt-quatre minutes." / "Neuf minutes.") pulls hard, and section 4's "Moins une. Moins deux." is propulsive. But catalogues, the Hamid aside, and the opaque backstory fragments ("Trois cent quatre-vingts. La moitié.") each cost me attention, and the direct address breaks the climax.

Recognition: 3. This gate passes, but weakly. The kessala's finger turns Khadija's ledger back on her, and the double's name answering turns her judgement back on itself, and I did feel my own scorekeeping implicated. But the story then states the recognition to me ("Vous aussi…"), and its emotional core (Fatiha, the earrings) stays too sealed for the ending to answer anything fully. A 4 needs that core to be felt, not withheld, and the authorial nudge removed.
