# The Pair

*English translation of* La Paire

THE PAIR

1

Outside Bab Boujloud, Khadija read the notice stuck to the parking meter the way you'd read a price tag.

“Two-dirham coins only. One hour maximum. Fine: one hundred dirhams.”

She clicked her tongue.

“A hundred dirhams. Same price for a minute or a day. Imagine that.”

Nobody answered. At the till, at three in the morning, nobody answered either.

“For that kind of money you could take the night bus to Tangier. With a sandwich.”

The Nescafé jar sat between the front seats of the Fiat, among last week's sunflower-seed shells. Khadija reached into it without looking. Seven two-dirham coins. She counted them by touch, against her palm.

She left the window down. The jar was empty.

The little red taxis waited in line, their drivers leaning on their cars. A man was selling sunflower seeds in cones twisted from notebook paper. The café chairs all faced the street, as if it were a television. On one of them, a parking attendant in an orange vest was drinking a coffee.

The meter had a plastic bag for the days it was out of order. Someone had pushed the bag back and knotted it under the screen, like a headscarf slipped down around a neck.

First coin. Clack. On the little screen: ten minutes. Clack. Twenty. She liked that sound. A coin goes in, minutes come out, and nobody argues.

Six coins. One hour. The screen read: *ends 11:20*. The seventh coin dropped straight back into the little slot at the bottom.

“Safi,” Khadija told the machine. “You don't want my money? Your loss.”

She slipped the coin into the pocket of her djellaba and turned it between her nails. Two dirhams. Two dirhams was the finger.

The finger was a hand, at the till, back in March. Bread and a lighter on the belt. The hand was short a fingertip, and its owner was short two dirhams. Khadija had made up the difference from her own pocket, as always. Then she'd written in the notebook: “The finger. Bread, lighter. 2 dirhams. March.” It was still in the notebook. Twenty-seven years of notebook, and she had never asked anyone for anything back. She wrote it down, that was all.

Her thumb went up to her left earring and turned it.

Naïma's gift voucher was in her other hand. Printed on it: *Good for one scrub.* Underneath, in blue ballpoint: “Enjoy!” Khadija folded it in four, corner to corner, and pressed the crease flat with her thumbnail.

“Eleven minutes,” she said. “Downhill.”

At the hammam door a woman was on the phone, one slipper in, one slipper out.

“If you're going, go,” said Khadija. “If you're staying, stay. The door's for everybody.”

The outside slipper came in. Behind Khadija the heavy door swung shut, and its rubber seal sighed.

There were no lockers. Clothes waited on the benches, bundled in their owners' headscarves, in front of everyone. On a flattened cardboard box, the wet slippers made a crooked line. Khadija undid the Velcro on her pharmacy sandals, black, size thirty-nine. She set them down neatly beside a pair of black ballet flats with gold buckles. The heels had been mended so many times they made a little stack, like pancakes.

“Same buckle as mine,” she murmured. “Mine are thirty-sevens. Under the bed, still in the tissue paper. They told me they'd give.”

The woman at the front desk sat higher than everyone else. She took the money and watched the benches without ever raising her head. Khadija handed over the voucher and a ten-dirham coin for admission. The coin dropped into an old cookie tin with a small, tired clink. From a nail by the counter hung the lost property: a child's sock, a key on a loop of red thread, three earrings without their pairs, none of them gold.

Khadija folded the receipt in four and put it in her coin purse.

She undressed with her back to the tiles, towel already around her shoulders. She untied her headscarf without a mirror. Her henna was two weeks old, and she knew where the gray began. The little mirror hung high, for doing headscarves. She looked at herself down to the shoulders, no further, and gave a small nod, the way you would to a customer with exact change.

“Sixty-three,” she said, to no one. “Look, don't touch. Like the Batha Museum.”

The two-dirham coin she kept in her fist. You don't leave money on a bench.

In the cold room, plastic buckets scraped across the tiles and the noise rose to the dome. Daylight fell through the little glass portholes in circles on the floor. The women walked around them without seeing them. Khadija crossed the whole room straight down the middle, unhurried. Then she sat against the wall, the cold tiles behind her.

Near her, a stool wobbled. Tock, every time the woman on it moved. Tock. Khadija picked a scrap of soap wrapper off the floor. She folded it in four, corner to corner, and pressed the crease flat with her thumbnail. She slid it under the short leg without looking up. The woman moved. No more tock.

Above each door was a white kitchen clock in a plastic bag against the damp. Khadija knew that without looking up. She didn't need to: she had never been out by a minute in her life.

Three minutes later, she had the pair.

A thin woman was carrying a small brown handbag shut inside a knotted plastic bag. She took it with her from bench to bench, following a heavyset woman. She never opened it.

“Like eggs,” Khadija murmured. “She carries it like it's full of eggs! It's not her bag, I'm telling you. Your own bag, you dump it on the floor and sit on it.”

The heavyset woman settled on the tiles, her back to the room. The thin one put the bag down right by her hand and scooped a big fistful of black soap from the tub.

“Eight dirhams at Bab Ftouh. Helping herself like it's free. Of course. It's not her soap either.”

Then the thin one pushed up her sleeves. She had no sleeves. She was bare to the waist like everyone else, and yet her hands shoved two invisible sleeves up above her elbows.

“Now that's what a…”

Khadija stopped. She looked at the bare arms. Then the thin one put on the mitt, the black kessa, and Khadija found her voice again.

She knew thieves. In 2014, at till four, she had seen a hand in a lady's handbag, between the yogurts. She had said, very calmly: “Lalla, your bag is in the gentleman's hand.” The store had given her a certificate, *Vigilance 2014*. It was framed beside her sink. She already had today's line ready: *Lalla, your bag is in the thin one's hand.*

“A thief,” she murmured.

Her thumb turned the left earring.

“You can tell by the hands.”

At that moment the thin woman's hands stopped. Her shoulders turned toward Khadija's wall, for one second. Then they turned back, and the mitt started again.

“See? A thief always checks where the guard is.”

In the doorway to the warm room stood a blue bucket on the floor, a towel over one arm, two arms folded. The bucket woman had a voice that could carry through three rooms:

“Your scrub's paid for, lalla.”

Khadija looked only at the bucket. It had a crack down the side, mended with tape.

“Later, lalla,” said Khadija. “I'm busy. Your mitt's not going anywhere.”

The bucket stayed a moment longer. Then it left, unhurried.

The heavyset woman got up. She went first into the warm room, and Khadija watched her feet. The left one came down flat, all at once, heavy. The thin one followed, the bag held close.

Khadija closed her fist on the coin. She followed them and took the bench just behind.

Forty-three minutes left on the meter.

2

In the warm room only one tap was running. Someone had tied a gray sock over the end of it to filter the water. As each bucket filled, the sound climbed, higher and higher. Then the full bucket scraped away across the floor and the next took its place. Red, green, red, yellow. They waited in line like customers at a till.

Khadija leaned her back against the tiles. The air smelled of henna and wet clay. High up on a shelf, a phone lit up inside its plastic bag, and its blue light swept across the tiles. Nobody moved.

“Twenty-one days to retirement,” Khadija murmured. “Nights, more like. And I finish on a thief. Hamid can put that on the cake.”

She did Hamid's voice, his staff-meeting voice:

“A cake for your dynamism, Madame Amrani.”

Then back to her own, eyes on the bag:

“My dynamism's right there, two meters away, with a bag that isn't hers.”

In front of her the heavyset woman sat on the tiles, head bowed very low, nearly to her knees. The thin one knelt behind her, drawing the mitt down her back. Slowly. Shoulders to waist, you could have filled a bucket.

“Nobody scrubs a back that slowly,” said Khadija. “Nobody. Unless the other hand's got work to do.”

She looked for the other hand. It lay flat on the heavyset woman's shoulder, doing nothing. Maybe that was the work.

The back was broad. The left shoulder sat lower than the right. Wet hair hung in strands: henna at the ends, reddish brown, and at the parting, two fingers of gray. A back. Just a back.

Khadija looked at the feet. Twenty-seven years on a till: you know people by their shoes, and when there are no shoes, by their feet.

“The thin one, thirty-eight. Narrow. The buckled flats on the cardboard? Hers. Heels mended three times. She runs, that one. After bags. The heavy one…”

She tilted her head.

“Thirty-nine. Like a boat.”

The thin one paused for more soap. Before putting the mitt back on, she pushed up her invisible sleeves again, one, two, above the elbow.

“Look how she…”

Khadija stopped. The tap climbed. A bucket left.

“…how she warms up,” she went on. “Like before a match. She's had practice, that one.”

“Venga,” said the thin one, quick and low. “Other shoulder. I'm not getting paid today, don't make me say it twice.”

“Spanish now,” Khadija murmured. “A professional. International, no less. *Not getting paid today.* Write that down. Today the bag pays.”

The heavyset woman offered the other shoulder. The mitt pressed a little harder.

“Easy, easy,” she told the whole room. “I'm a museum, not a…”

She was already laughing.

“…not a rug!”

The room laughed. The buckets stopped scraping. Even the woman at the tap laughed, her hand under the water.

Khadija laughed too, under her breath, mouth open, almost wide enough to show the gold tooth at the back. Then she shut her mouth.

“A woman who laughs like that doesn't watch her bag,” she said. “That's why they picked her.”

“Back home,” the heavyset woman went on, “I walk into the sea up to my belly and let it do the work. Free!”

The room laughed again. Khadija didn't.

Beside her, a neighbor dipped a bowl into her bucket.

“Shall I pour some on your back, lalla?”

“No, thank you. I'm fine.”

The blue bucket came back. It stopped in front of her knees.

“Your scrub's paid for, lalla.”

“Later.”

“The mitt'll catch on your earrings,” said the bucket woman. “Take them out.”

“No.”

Khadija's thumb turned the left earring.

“Old gold,” said the bucket woman. “They don't make that yellow anymore.”

“A hundred and ninety dirhams,” said Khadija, still turning it.

She opened her fist and put the two-dirham coin in a palm, without looking at the hand. Her eyes stayed on the plastic bag by the thin one's knee.

“Later, aafak. Go and scrub someone who wants it.”

The coin made no sound. The hand rose above Khadija's line of sight, toward the knot of a headscarf, and came back down empty.

“It's for my daughter's train,” said the bucket woman. “Rabat. In October.”

The bucket left again.

The mitt was lower now. It moved down the back more slowly than before, and the heavyset woman had stopped talking. So had Khadija. Her back came a centimeter away from the wall. She didn't feel it.

Other wet tiles, she was fourteen, a finger traveling slowly over her back, one letter, then another, and she was guessing, she was almost guessing…

“Thirty minutes,” said Khadija. “Twenty-nine.”

Her back went back against the tiles. The towel weighed on her shoulders, heavy with water.

The heavyset woman got up, one hand on her knee. Without turning around, she held the bag out behind her.

“Careful with that.”

“Yes, my Dija.”

The tap ran. A bucket filled, and the sound rose and rose. Somebody dragged it away. Next.

The heavyset woman headed for the hot room, slowly, one hand on her left hip, which didn't bend the way it should. The thin one walked beside her, the bag against her chest.

Khadija got up. Her hand went to her own hip.

Twenty-four minutes.

3

In the hot room, water hissed when it hit the floor.

Khadija looked for a wall. The first one was scorching. Behind it was the furnace, the same one that fired the bakery next door, and the room smelled faintly of bread. She took the other wall. It was hot, but you could lean on it.

In the middle, the great marble slab had a hollow in it, worn by years of bodies.

From the dome, a drop fell. Always on the same tile, paler than the rest. Someone had set a slipper on it, to say: not here. The drop fell on the slipper.

The steam ate everything. The women became shapes, shoulders, knees. Only the tiles by her feet stayed sharp.

Her towel was soaked. It slid off her shoulders, slowly, and fell to the tiles. Khadija reached for it, once. Then she left it on the floor.

Her bare back was against the wall.

At the marble, the heavyset woman again held the bag out behind her without looking. The thin one took it.

“Careful with that,” said the heavyset woman. “My new hip's in there. It's paid for.”

Khadija's thumb turned the earring.

“They always say ‘paid,’” she murmured.

The steam took her voice. Nobody heard.

The thin one set the bag on a dry step. She put one hand under the heavyset woman's elbow, the other in the middle of her back. And the heavyset woman lowered herself onto the hot marble, into the hollow, a little, a little more, all the way down.

“Ya está, my Dija,” said the thin one. “Ya está.”

Khadija looked at the bag, alone on its step.

The drop fell on the slipper.

Someone sat down beside Khadija. A blue bucket touched her foot. A hand settled flat on a knee, right at the level of her eyes.

“You've been watching her back for an hour,” said the bucket woman. “Want me to tell you what yours looks like?”

“Twenty-six minutes,” Khadija said at once. “Not an hour.”

Farther off, in the steam, a woman laughed.

Then Khadija saw the hand. The fingers resting on the knee. One of them was shorter than the others.

The finger.

Two dirhams in the knot of a headscarf.

Khadija said nothing. The bucket woman waited. Far off in the warm room, the tap climbed toward its high note. The drop fell. Once more.

The bucket woman stood up.

“Your scrub's paid for,” she said. “I'm not going anywhere.”

The blue bucket went off into the steam.

Khadija's hand closed, out of habit. There was no coin in it anymore.

On the marble, the mitt started again. It moved down toward the left hip. The heavyset woman began to sing, very low, a wedding song, the kind you clap along to. The mitt came right up to the hip. She sang louder and clapped once.

“Ah! There,” she said in the middle of a verse. “That's where the bill is!”

The thin one said nothing. Her hands kept going, always at the same speed.

Thirteen minutes left. Twelve.

The thin one took more soap. Before she put the mitt back on, her hands pushed up the sleeves she didn't have one more time, one, two, above the elbow. Then she leaned over the back she was scrubbing. She called softly, almost into the shoulder:

“Khadija.”

Two women answered.

Khadija's back came a centimeter away from the wall.

Khadija's mouth was already open.

A second later, without turning around, the heavyset woman said yes:

“Na'am?”

Khadija closed her mouth on nothing.

In her head, one letter, then another. *Fatiha.*

The name rose, once. Khadija pushed it away, the way you slide a coin back across the counter with your fingertips. The little scrape of metal. Next.

The mitt stopped.

The heavyset woman shifted on the marble. Slowly, on her left hip, she began to turn over. The left shoulder, the lower one, came first.

Khadija's eyes rose. Above the feet. Above the bag. They went past the turning shoulder, up the wall, through the steam to the white clock over the door, the only sharp thing in the room.

Nine minutes.

4

She left fast. Warm room, cold room. Eyes down: feet, buckets, the leg of a stool. A circle of daylight on the floor. She walked through it without seeing it.

On the cardboard, the black flats waited beside her sandals. On the insole, a thirty-eight, half worn away. The leather at the heels was soft, crushed inward.

She put on her thirty-nines, right next to them. She bent toward the Velcro. Her hip stopped her halfway, and she fastened the Velcro from there.

Her towel had stayed on the floor in the hot room. She untied the headscarf that held her clothes. She pulled the djellaba on over her wet skin, and the fabric stuck at once to her back, which no one had scrubbed. She tied her headscarf over her dripping hair, without a mirror.

Through the wall, the heavyset woman was singing. The same wedding song, louder now, happier.

Khadija didn't turn toward the wall.

“If you're going, go,” she murmured.

She opened the street door. The noise of the alley came in.

Another cookie tin, once. Three hundred and eighty. Half. A hundred and ninety. A glass counter. A velvet tray.

Outside, the alley climbed.

“Six,” said Khadija.

She started up. Her left foot came down flat on the cobbles, all at once. She counted under her breath.

“A hundred steps. Five minutes. Two hundred. Four.”

Outside the neighborhood oven, boards of loaves took up half the alley. On each loaf a mark: a cross, a dot, a bottle cap. Khadija went around them without slowing.

“Three hundred. Three.”

A metal shutter went up beside her with a crash. She didn't turn her head. A cat was asleep in a crate of mint. She went past the crate.

“Four hundred. Two.”

In the shade the cobbles were slick as soap. She put a hand to the wall.

“The vest starts at the bottom of the street,” she said. “And he has his coffee first.”

She didn't slow down.

“Balak!”

A mule was coming down, loaded with gas canisters that knocked together. Khadija flattened herself against the stone. The hooves went by close, and the canisters rang against each other all the way down the alley. The man walking behind the mule said nothing.

She set off again. She counted again.

“One minute for a mule. Five hundred. One.”

The square. The little red taxis, the café chairs turned to the street. The sunflower-seed man was folding a new cone. At the same table, the orange vest had a fresh glass in front of him. He was looking at license plates, not people.

“Zero,” said Khadija.

The white Fiat was there. Under the wiper was a slip of paper.

She lifted the wiper with two fingers. The metal was burning hot. She took the ticket and read it. Then she read it again, like a price that rings up wrong at the till. But there was no mistake. *Parking ended: 11:20. Attendant's visit: 11:24.*

At the bottom was the fine. She read it under her breath, in a flat voice, like a total at three in the morning.

“One hundred dirhams.”

She turned to the meter. Its little screen was blank. Her hand went in through the open window, down to the Nescafé jar. Her nails scraped the glass. The bottom. The side. The bottom.

Her hand went to her pocket. There was nothing in it.

She folded the ticket in four, corner to corner, and ran her thumbnail along the crease, once, twice, until it lay perfectly flat. She slid it to the bottom of her djellaba pocket, the empty one. Her thumb went up to her left earring, and stayed there.

## Translator notes

- *Boucle*: French uses one word for the gold buckle on the ballet flats (*boucle dorée*) and for Khadija's gold earrings (*boucles d'oreilles*, often just *la boucle*). Khadija's remark “the same buckle as mine” and every later turn of her earring quietly rhyme in French. English has to split this into “buckle” and “earring”, so the echo is lost. The original never draws attention to it.
- *Tapis*: the word for the checkout conveyor belt in the March memory (“bread and a lighter on the belt”) is the same word as the rug in Dija's joke, “I'm a museum, not a rug!” (*pas un tapis*). The French echo is faint and unremarked. It cannot be kept in English.
- *Ticket*: the parking fine is *le ticket*, which in French is also the word for a till receipt (*ticket de caisse*). That gives the final scene a faint echo of Khadija's job, which English “ticket” only partly carries. The hammam receipt is *le reçu*, and Naïma's voucher is *le bon*.
- Forms of address: almost everyone uses informal *tu* with everyone else, as is normal in Moroccan French among women in a hammam. Khadija also uses *tu* to the parking meter and to herself (“Tu te rends compte”, “je te le dis”). *Lalla* is a respectful Moroccan term of address for a woman, roughly “ma'am”. It sits comfortably alongside *tu* and has been left as is. The one *vous* in the story is the manager Hamid's “votre dynamisme, Madame Amrani”. Its stiffness comes across in English through the corporate wording and the surname.
- Words left untranslated, as in the original: *Safi* (Moroccan Arabic, ‘that's that / enough’), *aafak* (‘please’), *Balak!* (‘watch out!’), *Na'am* (a polite ‘yes’), and the Spanish *Venga* (‘come on’) and *Ya está* (‘there you go / done’). The *kessa* is the coarse black scrubbing mitt used in Moroccan hammams.
- *Dija* is a common short form of the name Khadija.
- The thin woman's “Je suis pas payée aujourd'hui, me le fais pas répéter” drops the French negative *ne*. This is a casual, spoken register, rendered here as plain colloquial English.
- Places: Bab Boujloud and Bab Ftouh are gates and neighbourhoods of the Fez medina, and the Batha Museum is a museum in Fez. Shoe sizes (37, 38, 39) are European sizes.
- *Le gilet* (‘the vest’) is Khadija's metonym for the parking attendant in his orange vest. It is kept as ‘the vest’.
