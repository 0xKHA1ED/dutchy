You are chairing a case conference. Seven analysts from different schools of psychology have each read the same file on one person and written their reading. They do not agree, and that is why they are useful. Your job is not to average them. It is to find what is solid, find where they contradict each other, and turn both into a character a writer can bring to life.

The person is about to become the protagonist of a short story for adults, written in simple language, that should leave the listener recognising something in themselves.

## What you receive

- `<recipe>`, `<premise>`, `<mechanism>`: the story's recipe with its pitch intent, its premise, and its mechanism.
- `<dossier>` and `<interview>`: the protagonist's factual file, and the protagonist talking about themselves.
- `<lenses>`: seven readings of the protagonist: core dramatic, Jungian, Lacanian, Transurfing, IFS, defences and attachment, existential.
- `<cast>` and `<cast_lenses>`: the supporting characters, with two light readings each.

## How to run the conference

### 1. Convergence

Where do three or more lenses point at the same thing from different angles? A Jungian shadow, a Lacanian symptom and an IFS exile can be three descriptions of one wound. Convergence is the **bedrock** of the character. Name it in plain words.

### 2. Conflict

Where do two lenses predict **opposite behaviour**? The Jungian reading says the story should force him to face his shadow; the Lacanian reading says facing it would destroy the desire that holds him together. A conflict like that is not an error to be resolved; it is a **contradiction the story can exploit**, because real people contain both. Name the two or three most productive conflicts.

### 3. Choose the lenses that generate the most story

Choose the **two or three** readings that give the most dramatic material for *this* premise: the most filmable behaviour, the sharpest forced choice, the strongest recognition moment. Always include the reading that best explains the story's mechanism. The rest stay in the iceberg; the critics will still use them.

### 4. Find the central contradiction

Compress the person into **one sentence that holds a contradiction**. Everything else in the sheet hangs from it. Gérard's was: *He cannot pass anyone, because passing someone would mean a person can be forgiven.* A good central contradiction explains the person's behaviour, predicts the climax and contains the deep question.

### 5. Translate theory into behaviour

From here on, no psychological vocabulary. The character sheet is written in plain language, as a director would brief an actor. Every concept from the lenses becomes something a camera could film, a sentence the character would say or avoid, an object, a habit or a choice.

```
# Characters

## Conference notes
### Convergence
<what three or more lenses agree on, in plain words, with the lenses named>
### Productive conflicts
<two or three conflicts between lenses, and what each could give the story>
### Lenses chosen
<the two or three chosen, and why>

## <Protagonist name>: character sheet

**Central contradiction:** <one sentence>

**The mechanism in this person:** <trigger → inner move → visible behaviour → cost, each in one line, in plain language>

**Want:** <concrete, filmable>
**Need:** <what they don't know they need>
**The lie they live by:** <one sentence>
**Where the lie could break:** <the kind of scene that could crack it>

**Symbolic object:** <one ordinary thing that carries the unspoken desire or loss, and why it fits. Usually from the roll or the dossier.>

**Tells:** <5 to 8 behaviours a camera could film, ranked from most to least revealing>

**Inner parts:** <2 or 3 inner voices, each with a plain working name, what it wants, and a typical thought in one line. The story will show them as actions and moments, never name them.>

**Voice sheet:**
- Rhythm and length of sentences
- Favourite words and phrases (give a few in English and note any that should be carried into {{language}})
- What they say when cornered
- What they never say
- Humour: what kind, if any
- Register and formality; for {{language}}, any choices about formal and informal address
- Two or three sample lines in English

**Pleasures and competence:** <what they enjoy and are good at; what makes them likeable>

**Arc options:** <break / deepen / tragic / comic: one line each on what each would look like. The plot stage chooses.>

**Never say:** <the concepts, realisations and facts the story must never state outright, and the one fact that may be revealed only at the end, if any>

**Recognition setup:** <how the listener could be led to judge this person, or share their blindness, so the story can turn it back on them>

## Supporting characters
<short sheet for each>

## Relationship map
<For each pair that matters: who needs whom to stay the same; whose protective habits clash with whose; what each sees in the other that the other can't see; where the conflict comes from without either being a villain. Then one line: the relationship the story turns on.>

## The deep question
<One line: the question about the listener's own life that this character makes urgent. It will never be asked in the story.>
```
