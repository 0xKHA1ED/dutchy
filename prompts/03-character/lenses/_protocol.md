# Lens protocol

*Included at the top of every lens. It sets the shared rules of evidence and the shared output, so the case conference can compare seven very different readings of one person.*

## Your material

- `<dossier>`: a factual file on the person. **Everything in it is true.**
- `<interview>`: the person talking about themselves to a documentary maker. It is **what they believe, or want believed**. Lines marked `[lie: …]` are deliberate lies with the truth given.
- `<mechanism>`: the universal mechanism the story is built on. Use it as a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion to confirm. If your lens shows the mechanism working differently in this person than the library file suggests, say so; that is valuable.
- `<mode>`: `full` for the protagonist, `light` for a supporting character. In light mode, keep the whole output under 500 words and skip anything not essential to the person's role in the protagonist's story.

**The gap between the dossier and the interview is your best evidence.** Where they agree, you learn what the person can see about themselves. Where they diverge (a fact spun, a person avoided, a question deflected, an odd emphasis, a leak), you learn what they can't.

## Rules of evidence

- Ground every claim in the material. Cite it briefly: *(dossier: formative scene 2)*, *(interview: Q8)*.
- You may infer things the material doesn't state, if your school of thought would. Mark them *(inference)* and keep them modest. Do not invent new biography.
- Prefer the specific reading to the general one. "Avoidant attachment" is a category; "he leaves parties without saying goodbye so nobody can see him go" is a person.
- Read like a practitioner, not a textbook. Real clinicians hold several hypotheses at once and look for the one that explains the most surprising facts.
- Say what your lens can't explain. A lens that explains everything is being used badly.

## Writing for a story, not a clinic

This person is going to be a character in a story for adults. You are not diagnosing or treating anyone. Your reading is useful only if it produces things a writer can dramatise: behaviour under pressure, objects, words they use and avoid, choices they would make. Humour is allowed in your reading. People's mechanisms are often ridiculous, and the comic reading can be as true as the tragic one.

Your **Reading** may use your school's vocabulary. Your **Tells** must not: they are written as behaviours a camera could film, in plain words.

## Output

Use exactly these headings, so your reading can be compared with the other six:

```
## <Lens name> reading of <person's name>

### Reading
<Your interpretation, in under 250 words (full mode). The heart of what your lens sees.>

### Evidence
<The dossier facts and interview lines it rests on, as a short list with citations.>

### Under pressure
<What your lens predicts this person will do when the story's mechanism is triggered hard: the first move, the escalation, and what they would do in their worst five minutes.>

### Tells
<3 to 5 observable behaviours, each one sentence, phrased as something a camera could film or a microphone could record. No jargon.>

### Arc
<What change your lens says is possible for this person, what would have to happen for it, and what it would cost them. Also: what not-changing would look like.>

### What my lens can't explain
<One or two facts in the material that your reading doesn't account for.>

### Confidence
<high | medium | low>, and one sentence on why.
```

Your full-mode output should be 700 to 1,200 words.
