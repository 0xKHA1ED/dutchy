# Two windows

**How it works:** One event, or one relationship, seen from two people's views in turn. The second view rewrites the first: what looked like cruelty was fear, what looked like love was rivalry, what seemed to be the protagonist's story was someone else's.

**Seen in:** *Rashomon*; *Gone Girl*; *The Affair*; *Atonement* (in part).

**Suits:** projection, mimetic desire, the scapegoat, envy as contempt.

## Beats

1. **Window one:** the event as the first person lives it. The listener forms judgements.
2. **The hinge:** a clear switch of perspective, marked for the listener (a new chapter, a name, a time).
3. **Window two:** the same event, or its lead-up, from the other side. The listener's judgements are tested.
4. **The overlap:** one moment seen from both sides, where the difference is starkest.
5. **After:** a short ending that belongs to neither, or to both.

## What it does to the listener

Recognition by complicity: the listener judged with the first narrator and is made to see what they missed. Very effective for projection stories.

## Notes for simple language

Make the switch impossible to miss in audio (a chapter break, a name, a different voice). Each window should have a distinct voice: rhythm, favourite words, formality.

## Common mistakes

- The second window simply proves the first one wrong. Better: both are partly right.
- Repeating too much of the event. Show only the moments where the views differ.
- Losing track of time in audio. Anchor each window with a clear marker.
