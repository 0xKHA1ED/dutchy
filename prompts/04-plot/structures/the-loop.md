# The loop

**How it works:** The same scene, ritual or situation happens three (sometimes four) times. The words and actions stay almost the same; the meaning changes each time because of what has happened in between, or because of one small variation. The last repetition is where the story lands.

**Seen in:** *Groundhog Day*; *Run Lola Run*; *Paterson*; many folk tales built on threes.

**Suits:** repetition compulsion, objet petit a, self-sabotage, rituals that protect a wound. Excellent for graded readers, because repetition recycles vocabulary naturally.

## Beats

1. **The first time:** the ritual or scene is established in detail.
2. **The second time:** nearly identical, but something has shifted: the listener now knows more, or one element is different.
3. **The third time:** the variation grows; the ritual is under pressure.
4. **The last time:** the same words, the opposite meaning, or the ritual broken by one small act.

## What it does to the listener

Comfort, then unease, then a jolt. The listener starts predicting the repetition, which makes the variation powerful.

## Notes for simple language

Ideal for B1: repeated sentences become anchors, and new vocabulary can be introduced through the variations. Keep the repeated passage short (three to six sentences) so it doesn't become tedious.

## Common mistakes

- Repetitions that are identical with no shift in meaning; the listener gets bored.
- Too many repetitions. Three is the classic number for a reason.
- A last repetition that explains its own change.
