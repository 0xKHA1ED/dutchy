# Level B1

B1 is the "independent user" level of the CEFR. A B1 learner can follow the main points of clear, standard language about familiar things, understand the description of events, feelings and wishes, and follow a story whose plot is clear. They still read slowly when sentences get long or vocabulary gets abstract.

The goal is not simple stories. It is **deep stories in simple language**: the depth lives in structure, subtext and what is left unsaid, and the words stay within reach. Hemingway and Carver wrote devastating stories in sentences a B1 learner could read.

These limits are guides for a writer, not a cage. A story that is technically perfect B1 and boring has failed; a story with five stretch words that a learner can work out from context has not.

## Grammar

| Use freely | Use with care | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Présent, passé composé, imparfait, futur proche (*aller* + infinitive), impératif | Futur simple; conditionnel présent (polite requests, wishes, hypotheses with *si* + imparfait); plus-que-parfait for brief flashbacks; subjonctif présent after the most common triggers (*il faut que, je veux que, avant que, pour que, bien que* rarely) | Passé simple; subjonctif passé and imparfait; conditionnel passé except in very common phrases (*j'aurais dû*); long chains of relative clauses |
| Pronouns *le, la, les, lui, leur, y, en*, one at a time | Two object pronouns together (*je le lui ai dit*) | Rare pronoun combinations; *dont* in complex structures (simple *dont* is fine) |
| Relative *qui, que, où* | *Ce qui, ce que, dont* | *Lequel, auquel, duquel* |
| Simple negation, *ne… plus, ne… jamais, ne… rien, ne… personne* | *Ne… que* | Literary *ne* alone (*je ne saurais*) |
| Passive avoided; *on* instead | Short passive (*il a été arrêté*) | Long passive constructions |

## Vocabulary

- **Core:** roughly the 2,500 most frequent words of French. Concrete nouns and common verbs carry most of the story.
- **Palette:** the 15 to 25 words chosen by the world stage for this story. They may be slightly above level, because they recur often enough to be learned.
- **Stretch budget:** beyond core and palette, at most about **1 word in 100** may be above level, and only when the scene makes its meaning obvious (it is described, shown, repeated, or contrasted with a known word). *Le double frein* is fine when the examiner presses his own brake and the car stops.
- **Abstract nouns** (*la culpabilité, la nostalgie, l'angoisse*) are rationed, which suits the house style anyway: feelings are shown, not named.
- **Idioms:** a few common ones are good and natural (*avoir le cafard, poser un lapin, en avoir marre*), especially in dialogue. Rare or regional idioms only when context explains them.

## Sentences

- Average sentence length roughly **8 to 14 words** in narration. Vary it: short sentences for tension, a longer one for calm or memory.
- At most **one subordinate clause** in most sentences. Two are acceptable occasionally; three is a sign to split.
- Clear subject–verb–object order. Avoid fronting long phrases before the subject.
- Plenty of dialogue. Dialogue is easier to follow, and it keeps the story alive.

## Length

Length follows the plot, but at B1 a chapter of 300 to 500 words (about 2 to 4 minutes of audio) is a comfortable unit. Most B1 stories will be 800 to 2,500 words in total.

## Comprehension aids that are allowed inside the story

- Recycling: key words return several times.
- Gloss by context: *Il a regardé dans le rétroviseur, le petit miroir au-dessus du volant.* Use this sparingly and naturally, never as a dictionary entry inside the prose.
- Clear chronology: if the story jumps in time, make the jump obvious (*Trente ans plus tôt…*).
