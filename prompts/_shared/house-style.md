# House style

These are the craft principles every story in this project is written to. The writer base includes this file in full; the critics use it to name what went wrong. Each principle comes with its reason, because a writer who understands why a rule exists will apply it in cases the rule never mentions.

## 1. Enact, don't announce

A feeling the listener is told about is a feeling they do not have. Let the listener infer anger from a door closed too carefully, love from a coat put on someone else's shoulders, guilt from a man who reads the local paper every day for a month.

- Characters rarely name their own emotions, and never precisely. Real people say "I'm fine" and mean five different things.
- Realisations are actions. She deletes the number. He leaves the key on the table. Nobody stands at a window and understands their life.
- The deep question of the story is never asked out loud, by anyone, including the narrator.

## 2. Subtext carries the weight

The most important thing in a scene is usually what nobody says. People talk about the dishes, the weather, the bus timetable, while the real conversation happens underneath. A simple line like "Tu as mangé ?" can be an apology, an accusation or a declaration of love. Subtext is also the reason simple language can carry a deep story: the words stay easy while the meaning gets heavy.

## 3. Specific beats general

Name the thing. Not "a car" but a grey car with one replaced side mirror. Not "she was nervous" but she tore the corners off receipts and kept them in her coat pocket. Specific details are what make a story feel observed rather than generated, and they are what the listener remembers.

The test for any detail: would only this person, in this place, do or notice this? If the answer is "anyone would", find another detail or cut it.

## 4. Every scene turns

At the end of a scene, something must be different from the start: safe has become unsafe, hope has become doubt, a secret is a little less secret. A scene where nothing turns is a scene the listener uses to check their phone. Cut it or make it turn.

## 5. Escalation comes from character

Trouble grows because of who the characters are. The defence that protects someone becomes the thing that traps them. Luck, accidents and outside villains may start trouble but never resolve it.

## 6. Contrast keeps feeling alive

A story at one emotional temperature goes numb. Put a laugh before the fall, a moment of warmth inside the dread, a stupid joke at a funeral. Dark stories need light to be seen.

## 7. Humour is a mode, not a genre

Humour comes from the same mechanisms as tragedy, played with a straight face: someone's self-deception followed further than is comfortable. Never wink at the listener. Characters who are funny without knowing it are funnier than characters who make jokes.

## 8. Repetition is a tool

In graded readers, repetition is how vocabulary is learned, and in fiction it is how meaning builds. Use refrains: a simple phrase or object that returns three or four times and means something different each time. The last return should land hardest.

## 9. Written for the ear

These stories are heard as often as read. The listener cannot look back up the page.

- Make it obvious who is speaking. Use speaker tags freely (*dit-elle*, *répond Gérard*); in audio they are not clutter, they are directions.
- Vary sentence length on purpose: short for tension, longer for calm and memory.
- End every chapter on a hook: a question, an image or a turn that makes stopping hard.
- Give each place one sound the listener can imagine.

## 10. Endings reframe

Every story needs a reframe, though not every story needs a twist: the final moment must change how the listener sees something earlier. Endings are enacted, never explained. The emotional question must be answered even when the plot is left open.

## 11. Trust the listener

Do not repeat a clue to make sure it was noticed. Do not explain a joke. Do not summarise what a scene meant. A listener who has to put something together will feel it; a listener who is told will only understand it.
