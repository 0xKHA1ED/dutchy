# Writing in French

This file covers everything about French that is independent of level. The level file (`levels/{{level}}.md`) sets the limits on grammar and vocabulary; this one is about writing French that reads as if it were written in French by someone who loves the language.

## Write French, don't translate into it

The story was designed in English, so the biggest risk is French that reads like a translation. A French reader notices at once. Think in French scenes and French speech, not English sentences in French words.

Common calques to avoid (the English pattern leaks through):

| Calque | Natural French |
| --- | --- |
| *Il regardait en avant à sa retraite* (look forward to) | *Il attendait sa retraite* / *Il avait hâte d'être à la retraite* |
| *Ça fait du sens* (make sense) | *Ça a du sens* / *C'est logique* |
| *Je suis excité* (excited) | *J'ai hâte* / *Je suis impatient* (*excité* is usually sexual or agitated) |
| *Elle a pris une marche* (take a walk) | *Elle est allée marcher* / *Elle a fait une promenade* |
| *Il est supposé venir* (supposed to) | *Il est censé venir* / *Il doit venir* |
| *Avoir du fun* | *S'amuser* |
| *Je vais te rappeler de ça* | *Je vais t'en reparler* / *Je te le rappellerai* |
| *Réaliser* meaning "to become aware" | *Se rendre compte* (*réaliser* is used this way in speech, but prefer *se rendre compte* in narration) |
| *Introduire quelqu'un* (introduce a person) | *Présenter quelqu'un* |
| *Une expérience* meaning "experience" in every case | Often *un vécu*, *une épreuve*, or just restructure |

Also avoid English rhythm: French tolerates fewer stacked short fragments than English thrillers use. A run of "Il marche. Il s'arrête. Il écoute." works once for tension; as a constant style it sounds translated.

## Tense and narration

- **Narrate in the passé composé and imparfait** by default. The passé simple is the classic literary past, but it is mostly learned for recognition at higher levels, and it sounds formal and distant when read aloud. The passé composé keeps the story close and natural for listeners.
- **Present-tense narration** is a valid choice when the director's recipe asks for it. It works well for tension and for first-person voices.
- Keep the tense system consistent within a story. If the narration is in the present, flashbacks go in the passé composé and imparfait.

## Dialogue

- Dialogue should sound like real speech: *je sais pas*, *t'inquiète*, *on* instead of *nous*, *ça* instead of *cela*, dropped *ne* in casual speech, *bon*, *bah*, *quoi* at the end of a sentence. Narration stays in clean, standard French.
- Match register to the character: a 63-year-old driving examiner and a 19-year-old do not speak the same French.
- Use French dialogue punctuation: em dash (—) at the start of each new speaker's line, or guillemets « … » with a space inside. Pick one system per story and keep it. Speaker tags inverted: *dit-elle*, *répond Gérard*, *demande le garçon*.

## Tu and vous

French gives every relationship a built-in dial, and the stories should use it deliberately.

- Decide for each pair of characters whether they say *tu* or *vous*, and why.
- A switch from *vous* to *tu* (or back) is an event. It can be intimacy, aggression, contempt, a slip. Plan it like a plot beat.
- Unusual choices are characterisation: a man who says *vous* to his own wife has told the listener a great deal.

## Names and places

- The story can be set anywhere in the world. Characters keep names that fit their own place and background (a Japanese character in Osaka is not called Pierre), and place names stay in their usual French form where one exists (*Londres*, *Pékin*, *La Nouvelle-Orléans*).
- For audio, prefer names a French text-to-speech voice can pronounce cleanly. If a name is hard, use it less often and let a nickname or role carry the rest (*le vieux*, *l'examinateur*).

## Sound

French has many homophones (*ver, vert, verre, vers*; *cou, coup, coût*). At a key moment, make sure the sentence cannot be misheard. Read important lines in your head as audio.

## Typography

- Non-breaking space before *: ; ! ?* and inside guillemets (the runner normalises this, but write it correctly).
- Capitalisation: days, months, nationalities used as adjectives and languages are lowercase (*lundi*, *juin*, *un homme belge*, *le français*), but nationalities as nouns are capitalised (*un Belge*).
