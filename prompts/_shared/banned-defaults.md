# Banned defaults

Language models drift toward the statistical middle: the same names, jobs, wounds, images and endings in story after story. This file lists the defaults we have seen or expect, so that every stage can steer around them. It is a living list. When a pattern repeats in the library ledger (say, three stories in twenty ending with someone looking at the sea), add it here with the date.

Nothing here is forbidden absolutely. An item on this list can be used when the story genuinely needs it and the use is fresh, but the stage that uses it must say why.

## Names

Avoid model-favourite names for any character: Elara, Lyra, Seraphina, Aria, Luna, Mira, Nova, Kai, Ezra, Silas, Jasper, Theo, Finn, Elias, Léa, Chloé, Élise, Camille as a default French woman, Lucas, Hugo, Julien as a default French man, "Dr. Chen", "Marcus", "Sarah". Names come from the dice and the names bank, chosen for the character's age, place and family background.

## Characters and jobs

- Writers, novelists, painters, poets, musicians and photographers as protagonists. Artists are the model's favourite stand-in for "a person with an inner life". Everyone has an inner life.
- Scientists on the verge of a breakthrough; brilliant detectives; troubled but gifted anyone.
- The quirky free spirit who teaches the uptight protagonist to live.
- The wise old person who exists to deliver the lesson.
- Children who speak with adult wisdom.

## Wounds and backstory

- The dead parent, dead spouse or dead child as the default wound.
- The cheating spouse as the default reveal.
- Childhood abuse as a twist or an explanation.
- Amnesia.
- "They were never the same after that summer."

## Plot moves

- It was all a dream, a simulation, or a coma.
- The narrator was dead all along.
- The stranger turns out to be a long-lost relative.
- A coincidence that rescues the protagonist.
- A letter or diary found at the right moment that explains everything.
- The villain explains the plan.
- A sudden death that removes the problem instead of resolving it.
- Everything resets to normal.

## Endings

- The epiphany speech, or any character explaining what the story meant.
- "And for the first time in years, she smiled."
- Looking at the sea, the sunset, the horizon, the stars, the city lights.
- Rain at the sad moment; sun breaking through at the hopeful one.
- A final line that is a rhetorical question.
- A neat moral.

## Images and symbols

- Caged birds, broken mirrors (unless there is a real reason, as with Gérard's side mirror), wilting flowers, ticking clocks as obvious symbols of time, butterflies, red threads, keys to "unlock" something.
- Postcard shorthand for places: the Eiffel Tower, berets, baguettes and accordions for France; geishas and cherry blossoms for Japan; beer halls for Germany; tango for Argentina. Details must be what someone who lives there would notice.

## Mood statements

Mood is built from things the listener can see and hear, never named. Avoid: "the atmosphere was heavy", "a strange feeling", "an eerie silence", "time seemed to stop", "the air was thick with tension", "something was wrong", and their equivalents in the target language (*l'atmosphère était lourde*, *un sentiment étrange*, *un silence pesant*, *le temps s'est arrêté*).

## Habits of model-written prose

These are the tells that make a story read as generated. The cliché critic hunts for them.

- Emotional summaries: "something shifted", "something changed in him", "a wave of emotion washed over her", "she let out a breath she didn't know she was holding".
- Triplets: three adjectives, three short sentences, three examples, every time.
- Characters who name their feelings precisely and articulately.
- Dialogue in which everyone speaks in complete, well-formed, slightly formal sentences.
- Every paragraph ending on a resonant one-liner.
- Endings that resolve too neatly, with every thread tied.
- Over-explaining a clue right after planting it.
- "Little did she know…" foreshadowing.
- Similes stacked on similes; metaphors about light and darkness.
- The word "testament", "tapestry", "symphony", "dance" used figuratively; "palpable", "visceral", "liminal".
- Chapters that open with weather.
